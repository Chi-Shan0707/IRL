A Gentle Introduction to Inverse Reinforcement Learning

What must be assumed, and what must be approximated — from forward RL to MaxEnt IRL, GAIL and AIRL, one derivation at a time.

Yuhan Chi· Fudan University· August 19, 2026· READING_TIME min read

This post is the web companion to the lecture handout of the same title (PDF notes; there is also a slide deck). It follows the handout section by section and keeps its numbering, so you can move freely between the two. The mathematics is identical; the web version adds room to breathe: the load-bearing derivations are broken into steps you can reveal one at a time, and every number in the worked examples can be checked by hand.

Abstract

Forward reinforcement learning is a well-posed problem: a reward determines an optimal value function uniquely, and a contraction mapping computes it. Inverse reinforcement learning is not well-posed, and it fails in two independent ways at once. It fails statistically, because infinitely many rewards explain the same behaviour, so no amount of data can single one out without extra assumptions. And it fails computationally, because every principled way of scoring a candidate reward requires an expectation under the policy that reward induces — an entire forward RL problem nested inside every gradient step.

This tutorial uses that pair as its spine. Each method is presented as a specific answer to two separate questions: what did you assume in order to make the answer unique? and how did you compute the inner expectation? Read this way, the sequence Max-Margin → MaxEnt → GAIL → AIRL stops looking like a list of tricks and becomes a single argument.

We develop maximum-entropy IRL first in the finite, known-model setting, where the partition function is an honest finite sum that a soft Bellman backward pass computes exactly. Only once we can point at the two places where $P(s'\mid s,a)$ is consumed do we ask what to do without it. Four points that secondary sources frequently get wrong receive particular care: the maximum-entropy objective needs a dynamics-induced reference measure from its very first line; naive maximum entropy is risk-seeking under stochastic dynamics and must be replaced by maximum causal entropy; GAIL's discriminator carries no reward information at convergence; and AIRL's transfer guarantee requires a state-only reward term.

How to read this tutorial

A first course in IRL usually presents four algorithms in historical order and leaves the reader to work out why each one replaced the last. This tutorial instead fixes two coordinates and locates every method on them.

The two coordinates

Coordinate 1 — the identification problem: what must be assumed?
Behaviour does not determine reward. The forward map $R\mapsto\pi^\star$ destroys information, and no estimator can recreate it. Every IRL method therefore smuggles in (i) a behaviour model saying how reward produces action, (ii) a selection principle choosing one representative from the surviving equivalence class, and (iii) a reward class restricting what $R$ may look like. These are modelling commitments, not discoveries. A method is only as trustworthy as they are.

Coordinate 2 — the computation problem: what must be approximated?
Every selection principle scores a candidate reward $R$ by comparing the expert's behaviour statistics with those of the policy that $R$ induces. Producing the second quantity means solving a forward RL problem inside the loop and then taking an expectation under its solution. In a finite MDP with a known model both steps are exact. Outside that setting they are not, and the history of IRL is largely the history of progressively cheaper surrogates for that one expectation.

The two coordinates are genuinely independent, and keeping them apart resolves most early confusion. A method can be statistically honest and computationally hopeless (exact tabular MaxEnt in a large MDP), or computationally excellent and statistically silent about reward (GAIL). When someone says “IRL is ill-posed”, ask which coordinate they mean.

The route

  • Part I establishes the forward direction and, more importantly, isolates the four properties that make it well-posed, so that we can watch each one fail in turn.
  • Part II attacks Coordinate 1 head-on: what precisely can and cannot be inverted, and therefore what the honest target of IRL is.
  • Part III develops max-margin and maximum-entropy IRL in the one regime where Coordinate 2 costs nothing — finite spaces, known $P$. This is the reference point for everything after it. The partition function is a finite sum; we compute it two ways and check that the numbers agree.
  • Part IV leaves that regime along its three natural exits, which are exactly the three questions beginners ask: finite → infinite, given → learned features, model-based → model-free.
  • Part V shows that GAIL and AIRL are the answers those three exits force on us, not an unrelated import from generative modelling.
  • Part VI puts every method back on the two coordinates in a single table.

Each method section closes with a Ledger box recording, in the method's own terms, what it assumed and what it approximated. If you remember nothing else, remember to fill in that ledger for every IRL paper you read.

Notation

Symbols are reused across the IRL literature with maddening inconsistency. We fix one convention here and keep it throughout. In particular: $P$ always denotes the transition kernel and never a distribution over trajectories (those are lower-case $p$); the discount is always $\gamma$, never $\lambda$; $\phi$ is always a per-step feature and $\Phi$ always its discounted trajectory sum; the entropy temperature is $\beta$; and the margin variable is $\xi$, so that $t$ is free to mean time and $k$ to mean an iteration index.

Part IThe well-posed direction

1Forward reinforcement learning

The purpose of this part is not to teach RL from scratch. It is to isolate why the forward problem is easy, in a form precise enough that we can later watch each reason fail.

1.1Markov decision processes

Definition (Markov decision process). A finite MDP is a tuple $(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},P,R,\gamma,d_0)$ where $\mathcal{S}$ and $\mathcal{A}$ are finite sets, $P(s'\mid s,a)$ is a transition kernel, $R:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}$ is bounded by $R_{\max}$, $\gamma\in(0,1)$, and $d_0\in\Delta(\mathcal{S})$. We write $\mathcal{M}$ for the reward-free part $(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},P,\gamma,d_0)$ and call it the dynamics.

Separating $\mathcal{M}$ from $R$ is not cosmetic. IRL is the problem of recovering $R$ given $\mathcal{M}$ (or given the ability to interact with it) and given behaviour. Everything hinges on how much of $\mathcal{M}$ we are allowed to know.

For a stationary policy $\pi$, the state and action values are

$$V^\pi(s)\triangleq\mathbb{E}_\pi\Big[\sum_{t=0}^\infty\gamma^tR(s_t,a_t)\,\Big|\,s_0=s\Big],\qquad Q^\pi(s,a)\triangleq R(s,a)+\gamma\sum_{s'\in\mathcal{S}}P(s'\mid s,a)\,V^\pi(s'),$$(1.1)

and the scalar objective is $J_R(\pi)\triangleq\mathbb{E}_{s_0\sim d_0}[V^\pi(s_0)]$.

1.2Policy evaluation is a linear solve

Fix $\pi$ and enumerate the states $s_1,\ldots,s_n$ with $n=|\mathcal{S}|$. Collect the values in a vector $\mathbf{V}^\pi\in\mathbb{R}^n$, and define the induced reward vector and the induced state-to-state transition matrix by averaging over the policy's action choice:

$$[\mathbf{R}^\pi]_i=\sum_a\pi(a\mid s_i)\,R(s_i,a),\qquad [\mathbf{P}^\pi]_{ij}=\sum_a\pi(a\mid s_i)\,P(s_j\mid s_i,a).$$

The Bellman expectation equation — “value now equals reward now plus discounted value next” — is then a linear system:

$$\mathbf{V}^\pi=\mathbf{R}^\pi+\gamma\,\mathbf{P}^\pi\mathbf{V}^\pi.$$(1.2)

Theorem (exact policy evaluation). For $\gamma\in(0,1)$ and row-stochastic $\mathbf{P}^\pi$, the matrix $\mathbf{I}-\gamma\mathbf{P}^\pi$ is invertible and

$$\mathbf{V}^\pi=(\mathbf{I}-\gamma\mathbf{P}^\pi)^{-1}\mathbf{R}^\pi=\sum_{k=0}^\infty\gamma^k(\mathbf{P}^\pi)^k\,\mathbf{R}^\pi.$$(1.3)
Proof, step 1: the transition matrix has unit norm

Each row of $\mathbf{P}^\pi$ is a probability distribution: non-negative entries summing to one. The induced $\infty$-norm of a matrix is its largest absolute row sum, so $\|\mathbf{P}^\pi\|_\infty=1$ and therefore $\|\gamma\mathbf{P}^\pi\|_\infty=\gamma<1$.

Proof, step 2: the Neumann series converges

Since $\|(\gamma\mathbf{P}^\pi)^k\|_\infty\le\gamma^k$, the series $\sum_{k\ge0}\gamma^k(\mathbf{P}^\pi)^k$ converges absolutely. Multiplying it by $\mathbf{I}-\gamma\mathbf{P}^\pi$ telescopes:

$$(\mathbf{I}-\gamma\mathbf{P}^\pi)\sum_{k=0}^{K}\gamma^k(\mathbf{P}^\pi)^k=\mathbf{I}-\gamma^{K+1}(\mathbf{P}^\pi)^{K+1}\;\xrightarrow{K\to\infty}\;\mathbf{I},$$

so the series is the inverse $(\mathbf{I}-\gamma\mathbf{P}^\pi)^{-1}$.

Proof, step 3: solve the linear system

Rearranging (1.2) gives $(\mathbf{I}-\gamma\mathbf{P}^\pi)\mathbf{V}^\pi=\mathbf{R}^\pi$; apply the inverse from step 2 to both sides. The solution exists and is unique. ∎

Remark: value is an occupancy-weighted sum of rewards

The Neumann series has a reading we will need again and again. Entry $(i,j)$ of $(\mathbf{P}^\pi)^k$ is the probability of being in $s_j$ after $k$ steps when starting from $s_i$, so

$$\big[(\mathbf{I}-\gamma\mathbf{P}^\pi)^{-1}\big]_{ij}=\sum_{k\ge0}\gamma^k\Pr_\pi(s_k=s_j\mid s_0=s_i).$$

Up to the normalisation factor $(1-\gamma)$, this is the matrix of discounted state occupancies, and (1.3) says that value is an occupancy-weighted sum of rewards. That single observation is the bridge from classical IRL to GAIL; we develop it properly in Section 5.9 and Section 9.

1.3Optimality is a fixed point

Definition (Bellman optimality operator).

$$(\mathcal{B}^\star V)(s)\triangleq\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\Big[R(s,a)+\gamma\sum_{s'}P(s'\mid s,a)\,V(s')\Big].$$

Theorem (contraction). $\|\mathcal{B}^\star V_1-\mathcal{B}^\star V_2\|_\infty\le\gamma\|V_1-V_2\|_\infty$. Consequently $\mathcal{B}^\star$ has a unique fixed point $V^\star$, value iteration converges geometrically,

$$\|V^{(k)}-V^\star\|_\infty\le\gamma^k\,\|V^{(0)}-V^\star\|_\infty,$$

and any greedy policy with respect to $V^\star$ is optimal.

Proof, step 1: a max is 1-Lipschitz

For any two functions $f,g$ on a finite set, $|\max_af(a)-\max_ag(a)|\le\max_a|f(a)-g(a)|$. (If the left maximum is attained at $a_1$, then $\max f-\max g\le f(a_1)-g(a_1)$; argue symmetrically for the other sign.)

Proof, step 2: apply it state by state

Fix $s$ and let $f(a),g(a)$ be the bracketed terms for $V_1$ and $V_2$. The rewards cancel:

$$f(a)-g(a)=\gamma\sum_{s'}P(s'\mid s,a)\big(V_1(s')-V_2(s')\big).$$

Because $\sum_{s'}P(s'\mid s,a)=1$, this is a weighted average of differences, so $|f(a)-g(a)|\le\gamma\|V_1-V_2\|_\infty$. Taking the maximum over $s$ gives the contraction.

Proof, step 3: Banach's fixed-point theorem

$\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}|}$ with the $\infty$-norm is complete, so a $\gamma$-contraction has exactly one fixed point, and iterating from any $V^{(0)}$ approaches it at rate $\gamma^k$. Acting greedily with respect to $V^\star$ attains the maximum in $\mathcal{B}^\star$ at every state, so the greedy policy's value is also a fixed point of $\mathcal{B}^\star$, hence equal to $V^\star$. ∎

1.4Why the forward direction is easy, in four bullets

The four pillars of forward RL
  1. The unknown is finite-dimensional and the equations are exact. $V^\star\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}|}$ solves a finite system.
  2. The solution operator is a contraction. Uniqueness and a geometric convergence rate come for free.
  3. Expectations are finite sums over a known kernel. Every $\sum_{s'}P(s'\mid s,a)(\cdot)$ can be evaluated exactly.
  4. The objective is given. There is a ground truth against which any answer can be scored.

Pillar 4 is what IRL removes, and that is Coordinate 1. Pillars 1–3 are what large-scale or unknown environments remove, and that is Coordinate 2. Forward RL already has a well-developed answer to losing pillars 1–3, which we recall briefly, because IRL inherits it wholesale.

1.5Model-based and model-free forward RL

  1. Model-based. $P$ and $R$ are known. Solve by (1.3), or by value or policy iteration. Value iteration converges linearly with factor $\gamma$; policy iteration terminates after finitely many steps (there are at most $|\mathcal{A}|^{|\mathcal{S}|}$ deterministic policies) and is typically far faster in practice.
  2. Model-free. $P$ is unknown; the agent may only sample transitions. Temporal-difference methods ($Q$-learning, SARSA) replace the exact expectation $\sum_{s'}P(\cdot)$ by sampled successors. Tabular $Q$-learning converges almost surely to $Q^\star$ under the Robbins–Monro conditions $\sum_t\alpha_t=\infty$, $\sum_t\alpha_t^2<\infty$, provided every $(s,a)$ is visited infinitely often [Watkins & Dayan 1992; Tsitsiklis 1994]. With function approximation — policy-gradient methods included [Sutton & Barto 2018] — the combination of bootstrapping, off-policy sampling and approximation, known as the deadly triad, can destroy the contraction, and neither uniqueness nor convergence is guaranteed.
Remark: ill-conditioning as $\gamma\to1$

In the $\infty$-norm, $\kappa_\infty(\mathbf{I}-\gamma\mathbf{P}^\pi)\le\frac{1+\gamma}{1-\gamma}$, so the linear solve degrades as $\gamma\to1^-$. The same $(1-\gamma)^{-1}$ factors reappear in sample-complexity bounds: obtaining an $\varepsilon$-optimal policy with a generative model costs $\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\big(\frac{|\mathcal{S}||\mathcal{A}|}{\varepsilon^2(1-\gamma)^3}\big)$ samples [Azar et al. 2013]. The effective horizon $(1-\gamma)^{-1}$ is the quantity that hurts, in every regime, forward and inverse alike.

Part IIThe ill-posed direction

2Setting up the inverse problem

In many domains — driving, surgery, aerobatic flight, dialogue — writing down a reward is harder than demonstrating the behaviour. Hand-specified rewards are routinely mis-specified, and optimising a mis-specified reward produces the failure mode known as reward hacking. IRL proposes to infer the objective from behaviour instead.

The two directions $$\begin{aligned} &\textbf{Forward RL:}\quad \text{dynamics } \mathcal{M} \;+\; \text{reward } R \;\xrightarrow{\ \text{optimise}\ }\; \text{optimal policy } \pi^\star\\[4pt] &\textbf{Inverse RL:}\quad \text{dynamics } \mathcal{M} \;+\; \text{demonstrations } \mathcal{D}_E \;\xrightarrow{\ \text{infer}\ }\; \text{reward } R \ \text{(then, usually, } \pi) \end{aligned}$$

We observe $m$ trajectories $\mathcal{D}_E=\{\tau^{(1)},\ldots,\tau^{(m)}\}$, with $\tau=(s_0,a_0,s_1,a_1,\ldots,s_T)$, and define the discounted trajectory return $R(\tau)\triangleq\sum_{t\ge0}\gamma^tR(s_t,a_t)$.

Assumption (expert near-optimality). $\mathcal{D}_E$ was generated by a policy $\pi_E$ that is optimal, or near-optimal, for some unknown reward $R^\star$ in the given dynamics $\mathcal{M}$.

This assumption is the behaviour model in its crudest form. Taken literally, it yields only inequalities:

$$J_R(\pi_E)\;\ge\;J_R(\pi)\qquad\text{for all }\pi\in\Pi.$$(2.1)

A system of inequalities is a weak thing to estimate from: it has no notion of “almost satisfied”, and a single noisy demonstration can make it infeasible. Much of the technical content of modern IRL consists of replacing (2.1) by a probabilistic behaviour model, which turns inequalities into a likelihood and therefore into something a gradient can act on. We will watch exactly that happen in Section 5.

3What exactly can be inverted?

Calling IRL “inverse optimisation” invites the wrong intuition, because ordinary inversion presumes injectivity. The forward map

$$R\;\longmapsto\;\Pi^\star(R)=\{\text{policies optimal for }R\}$$(3.1)

is drastically many-to-one. Before choosing an algorithm we should know precisely what information this map destroys.

Three objects that must not be confused
  1. $V^\star$ is unique, given $\mathcal{M}$ and $R$: it is the fixed point of a contraction.
  2. $\pi^\star$ need not be unique: ties in the greedy step give a set $\Pi^\star(R)$.
  3. $R$ is far from unique, given behaviour: $\Pi^\star$ has enormous fibres.

Uniqueness of the forward solution says nothing whatever about uniqueness of the inverse. These three statements are about three different maps.

3.1The solution set is a polyhedral cone

Ng and Russell gave the exact characterisation for a finite MDP and a fixed deterministic policy.

Theorem (consistent rewards; Ng & Russell 2000). Let $\mathcal{S}$ be finite, let $R\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}|}$ be a state-only reward, and let $\mathbf{P}_a$ denote the matrix with entries $P(s'\mid s,a)$. A deterministic policy $\pi$, relabelled so that $\pi(s)\equiv a_1$, is optimal for $R$ if and only if

$$(\mathbf{P}_{a_1}-\mathbf{P}_a)(\mathbf{I}-\gamma\mathbf{P}_{a_1})^{-1}R\;\succeq\;0\qquad\text{for all }a\in\mathcal{A}\setminus\{a_1\}.$$(3.2)

Read (3.2) with Section 1.2 in mind: $(\mathbf{I}-\gamma\mathbf{P}_{a_1})^{-1}R$ is the value vector of $\pi$, and $(\mathbf{P}_{a_1}-\mathbf{P}_a)$ applied to it compares the expected next-state value of the expert's action with that of any deviation. The condition is linear in $R$, which has two immediate consequences.

Corollary (a convex cone containing zero). The set of rewards consistent with an observed policy is a closed convex cone: it is closed under non-negative scaling and under addition. In particular it always contains $R\equiv0$, for which every policy is optimal, so observing behaviour alone can never exclude the degenerate explanation “nothing matters”.

Beyond $R\equiv0$, any constant $R\equiv c$ makes all policies optimal. A subtler and far more important degeneracy is potential-based shaping: for any potential $\Psi:\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}$, the shaped reward

$$R'(s,a,s')=R(s,a)+\gamma\Psi(s')-\Psi(s)$$(3.3)

satisfies $J_{R'}(\pi)=J_R(\pi)-\mathbb{E}_{s_0\sim d_0}[\Psi(s_0)]$ for every $\pi$. The shift is the same for all policies, so the entire preference ordering over policies is preserved and $\Pi^\star(R')=\Pi^\star(R)$. The calculation is a telescoping sum:

Step 1: split the shaped return

By linearity of expectation, the shaped return is the original return plus a shaping term:

$$J_{R'}(\pi)=J_R(\pi)+\mathbb{E}_\pi\Big[\sum_{t\ge0}\big(\gamma^{t+1}\Psi(s_{t+1})-\gamma^t\Psi(s_t)\big)\Big].$$
Step 2: the partial sums telescope

Every intermediate potential appears once with a plus sign and once with a minus sign:

$$\sum_{t=0}^{N-1}\big(\gamma^{t+1}\Psi(s_{t+1})-\gamma^t\Psi(s_t)\big)=\gamma^N\Psi(s_N)-\Psi(s_0).$$
Step 3: take the limit

$\Psi$ is bounded on a finite state space and $\gamma^N\to0$, so the shaping term converges to $-\Psi(s_0)$ along every trajectory. Taking expectations, $J_{R'}(\pi)=J_R(\pi)-\mathbb{E}_{s_0\sim d_0}[\Psi(s_0)]$.

Step 4: the offset does not depend on the policy

The initial distribution $d_0$ is fixed by the dynamics, not chosen by the agent. Every policy is shifted by the same constant, so every comparison $J(\pi_1)\gtrless J(\pi_2)$ is unchanged, and $R$ and $R'$ have exactly the same optimal policies. No amount of behavioural data can tell them apart.

Remark: correct attribution

The optimality-preserving property of potential-based shaping is due to Ng, Harada and Russell (1999), not to the 2000 IRL paper. The 1999 paper also proves the converse: potential-based shaping is the only form of additive reward modification that preserves optimality for all transition kernels. That converse is exactly why potential shaping is the right notion of “nuisance parameter” in IRL, and it is the ambiguity that AIRL sets out to quotient away in Section 11.

3.2A minimal example

Consider a single state and a single decision between left and right; the expert chooses left. All we learn is

$$R(s,\texttt{left})\;\ge\;R(s,\texttt{right}).$$(3.4)

The pairs $(1,0)$, $(10,-3)$ and $(0,-100)$ are equally consistent. One observation cannot pin down two numbers, and repeating the same observation does not help: additional demonstrations add inequalities, which sharpen the preference structure but never fix an absolute scale or offset. Notice, though, that the example is already informative in one respect: it rules out $R(s,\texttt{left})<R(s,\texttt{right})$. IRL is not hopeless; it is partially identifying.

3.3The honest target

Given all this, the scientifically defensible outputs of IRL are:

  1. an equivalence class of rewards inducing the observed behaviour;
  2. a single representative chosen by an explicit selection principle (maximum margin, maximum entropy, a Bayesian prior, sparsity);
  3. or simply a policy whose occupancy measure matches the expert's, with no claim about reward at all.

Item 3 is imitation learning, and it is often what one actually wants. Item 2 is what most IRL papers deliver. Item 1 is what the data actually supports. Confusing 2 with 1 is the single most common overclaim in the literature. Recent work makes the partial-identifiability picture precise and shows that even with infinite data and a known model, the reward is only determined up to explicitly characterisable transformations [Cao et al. 2021; Skalse et al. 2023].

A claim to be suspicious of

“Our method recovers the true reward function.” No method recovers a reward function from behaviour alone. Methods recover a representative of an equivalence class, selected by an assumption the paper made. The useful question is never “is the reward right?” but “is the reward right for the use I have in mind?” The two most common uses — reproducing behaviour in the same MDP, and transferring behaviour to a different MDP — need very different amounts of identification. Section 11 is entirely about the gap between them.

3.4Choosing a reward class

The unknown $R:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}$ is a function; if $\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}$ is continuous it is an infinite-dimensional object. A finite vector $w\in\mathbb{R}^d$ appears only after we choose a model class, most commonly the linear one

$$R_w(s,a)=w^\top\phi(s,a).$$(3.5)

This is a modelling commitment on Coordinate 1, not a law of IRL. Alternatives include kernel and Gaussian-process rewards, neural rewards $R_\theta$, and reward classes defined implicitly by a discriminator architecture. Whether $\phi$ must be hand-designed is taken up in full in Section 7; for now we record what (3.5) buys, because the next two sections lean on it entirely.

Proposition (linearity collapses return into a finite statistic). Under (3.5),

$$J_{R_w}(\pi)=\mathbb{E}_\pi\Big[\sum_{t\ge0}\gamma^tw^\top\phi(s_t,a_t)\Big]=w^\top\,\mathbb{E}_\pi\Big[\sum_{t\ge0}\gamma^t\phi(s_t,a_t)\Big]=w^\top\mu(\pi).$$(3.6)

The vector $\mu(\pi)\in\mathbb{R}^d$ is a sufficient statistic of the policy for the purpose of evaluating any reward in the class: everything else about $\pi$ is irrelevant to $w$. This algebraic fact is what makes classical IRL tractable, and losing it is the price of leaving the linear class.
