A Gentle Introduction to Inverse Reinforcement Learning

What must be assumed, and what must be approximated — from forward RL to MaxEnt IRL, GAIL and AIRL, one derivation at a time.

Yuhan Chi· Fudan University· August 19, 2026· About 70 min read

This post is the web companion to the lecture handout of the same title (PDF notes; there is also a slide deck). It follows the handout section by section and keeps its numbering, so you can move freely between the two. The mathematics is identical; the web version adds room to breathe: the load-bearing derivations are broken into steps you can reveal one at a time, and every number in the worked examples can be checked by hand.

Abstract

Forward reinforcement learning is a well-posed problem: a reward determines an optimal value function uniquely, and a contraction mapping computes it. Inverse reinforcement learning is not well-posed, and it fails in two independent ways at once. It fails statistically, because infinitely many rewards explain the same behaviour, so no amount of data can single one out without extra assumptions. And it fails computationally, because every principled way of scoring a candidate reward requires an expectation under the policy that reward induces — an entire forward RL problem nested inside every gradient step.

This tutorial uses that pair as its spine. Each method is presented as a specific answer to two separate questions: what did you assume in order to make the answer unique? and how did you compute the inner expectation? Read this way, the sequence Max-Margin → MaxEnt → GAIL → AIRL stops looking like a list of tricks and becomes a single argument.

We develop maximum-entropy IRL first in the finite, known-model setting, where the partition function is an honest finite sum that a soft Bellman backward pass computes exactly. Only once we can point at the two places where $P(s'\mid s,a)$ is consumed do we ask what to do without it. Four points that secondary sources frequently get wrong receive particular care: the maximum-entropy objective needs a dynamics-induced reference measure from its very first line; naive maximum entropy is risk-seeking under stochastic dynamics and must be replaced by maximum causal entropy; GAIL's discriminator carries no reward information at convergence; and AIRL's transfer guarantee requires a state-only reward term.

How to read this tutorial

A first course in IRL usually presents four algorithms in historical order and leaves the reader to work out why each one replaced the last. This tutorial instead fixes two coordinates and locates every method on them.

The two coordinates

Coordinate 1 — the identification problem: what must be assumed?
Behaviour does not determine reward. The forward map $R\mapsto\pi^\star$ destroys information, and no estimator can recreate it. Every IRL method therefore smuggles in (i) a behaviour model saying how reward produces action, (ii) a selection principle choosing one representative from the surviving equivalence class, and (iii) a reward class restricting what $R$ may look like. These are modelling commitments, not discoveries. A method is only as trustworthy as they are.

Coordinate 2 — the computation problem: what must be approximated?
Every selection principle scores a candidate reward $R$ by comparing the expert's behaviour statistics with those of the policy that $R$ induces. Producing the second quantity means solving a forward RL problem inside the loop and then taking an expectation under its solution. In a finite MDP with a known model both steps are exact. Outside that setting they are not, and the history of IRL is largely the history of progressively cheaper surrogates for that one expectation.

The two coordinates are genuinely independent, and keeping them apart resolves most early confusion. A method can be statistically honest and computationally hopeless (exact tabular MaxEnt in a large MDP), or computationally excellent and statistically silent about reward (GAIL). When someone says “IRL is ill-posed”, ask which coordinate they mean.

The route

  • Part I establishes the forward direction and, more importantly, isolates the four properties that make it well-posed, so that we can watch each one fail in turn.
  • Part II attacks Coordinate 1 head-on: what precisely can and cannot be inverted, and therefore what the honest target of IRL is.
  • Part III develops max-margin and maximum-entropy IRL in the one regime where Coordinate 2 costs nothing — finite spaces, known $P$. This is the reference point for everything after it. The partition function is a finite sum; we compute it two ways and check that the numbers agree.
  • Part IV leaves that regime along its three natural exits, which are exactly the three questions beginners ask: finite → infinite, given → learned features, model-based → model-free.
  • Part V shows that GAIL and AIRL are the answers those three exits force on us, not an unrelated import from generative modelling.
  • Part VI puts every method back on the two coordinates in a single table.

Each method section closes with a Ledger box recording, in the method's own terms, what it assumed and what it approximated. If you remember nothing else, remember to fill in that ledger for every IRL paper you read.

Symbols are reused across the IRL literature with maddening inconsistency. We fix one convention here and keep it throughout. In particular: $P$ always denotes the transition kernel and never a distribution over trajectories (those are lower-case $p$); the discount is always $\gamma$, never $\lambda$; $\phi$ is always a per-step feature and $\Phi$ always its discounted trajectory sum; the entropy temperature is $\beta$; and the margin variable is $\xi$, so that $t$ is free to mean time and $k$ to mean an iteration index.

Part IThe well-posed direction

1Forward reinforcement learning

The purpose of this part is not to teach RL from scratch. It is to isolate why the forward problem is easy, in a form precise enough that we can later watch each reason fail.

1.1Markov decision processes

Definition (Markov decision process). A finite MDP is a tuple $(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},P,R,\gamma,d_0)$ where $\mathcal{S}$ and $\mathcal{A}$ are finite sets, $P(s'\mid s,a)$ is a transition kernel, $R:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}$ is bounded by $R_{\max}$, $\gamma\in(0,1)$, and $d_0\in\Delta(\mathcal{S})$. We write $\mathcal{M}$ for the reward-free part $(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},P,\gamma,d_0)$ and call it the dynamics.

Separating $\mathcal{M}$ from $R$ is not cosmetic. IRL is the problem of recovering $R$ given $\mathcal{M}$ (or given the ability to interact with it) and given behaviour. Everything hinges on how much of $\mathcal{M}$ we are allowed to know.

For a stationary policy $\pi$, the state and action values are

$$V^\pi(s)\triangleq\mathbb{E}_\pi\Big[\sum_{t=0}^\infty\gamma^tR(s_t,a_t)\,\Big|\,s_0=s\Big],\qquad Q^\pi(s,a)\triangleq R(s,a)+\gamma\sum_{s'\in\mathcal{S}}P(s'\mid s,a)\,V^\pi(s'),$$(1.1)

and the scalar objective is $J_R(\pi)\triangleq\mathbb{E}_{s_0\sim d_0}[V^\pi(s_0)]$.

1.2Policy evaluation is a linear solve

Fix $\pi$ and enumerate the states $s_1,\ldots,s_n$ with $n=|\mathcal{S}|$. Collect the values in a vector $\mathbf{V}^\pi\in\mathbb{R}^n$, and define the induced reward vector and the induced state-to-state transition matrix by averaging over the policy's action choice:

$$[\mathbf{R}^\pi]_i=\sum_a\pi(a\mid s_i)\,R(s_i,a),\qquad [\mathbf{P}^\pi]_{ij}=\sum_a\pi(a\mid s_i)\,P(s_j\mid s_i,a).$$

The Bellman expectation equation — “value now equals reward now plus discounted value next” — is then a linear system:

$$\mathbf{V}^\pi=\mathbf{R}^\pi+\gamma\,\mathbf{P}^\pi\mathbf{V}^\pi.$$(1.2)

Theorem (exact policy evaluation). For $\gamma\in(0,1)$ and row-stochastic $\mathbf{P}^\pi$, the matrix $\mathbf{I}-\gamma\mathbf{P}^\pi$ is invertible and

$$\mathbf{V}^\pi=(\mathbf{I}-\gamma\mathbf{P}^\pi)^{-1}\mathbf{R}^\pi=\sum_{k=0}^\infty\gamma^k(\mathbf{P}^\pi)^k\,\mathbf{R}^\pi.$$(1.3)
Proof, step 1: the transition matrix has unit norm

Each row of $\mathbf{P}^\pi$ is a probability distribution: non-negative entries summing to one. The induced $\infty$-norm of a matrix is its largest absolute row sum, so $\|\mathbf{P}^\pi\|_\infty=1$ and therefore $\|\gamma\mathbf{P}^\pi\|_\infty=\gamma<1$.

Proof, step 2: the Neumann series converges

Since $\|(\gamma\mathbf{P}^\pi)^k\|_\infty\le\gamma^k$, the series $\sum_{k\ge0}\gamma^k(\mathbf{P}^\pi)^k$ converges absolutely. Multiplying it by $\mathbf{I}-\gamma\mathbf{P}^\pi$ telescopes:

$$(\mathbf{I}-\gamma\mathbf{P}^\pi)\sum_{k=0}^{K}\gamma^k(\mathbf{P}^\pi)^k=\mathbf{I}-\gamma^{K+1}(\mathbf{P}^\pi)^{K+1}\;\xrightarrow{K\to\infty}\;\mathbf{I},$$

so the series is the inverse $(\mathbf{I}-\gamma\mathbf{P}^\pi)^{-1}$.

Proof, step 3: solve the linear system

Rearranging (1.2) gives $(\mathbf{I}-\gamma\mathbf{P}^\pi)\mathbf{V}^\pi=\mathbf{R}^\pi$; apply the inverse from step 2 to both sides. The solution exists and is unique. ∎

Remark: value is an occupancy-weighted sum of rewards

The Neumann series has a reading we will need again and again. Entry $(i,j)$ of $(\mathbf{P}^\pi)^k$ is the probability of being in $s_j$ after $k$ steps when starting from $s_i$, so

$$\big[(\mathbf{I}-\gamma\mathbf{P}^\pi)^{-1}\big]_{ij}=\sum_{k\ge0}\gamma^k\Pr_\pi(s_k=s_j\mid s_0=s_i).$$

Up to the normalisation factor $(1-\gamma)$, this is the matrix of discounted state occupancies, and (1.3) says that value is an occupancy-weighted sum of rewards. That single observation is the bridge from classical IRL to GAIL; we develop it properly in Section 5.9 and Section 9.

1.3Optimality is a fixed point

Definition (Bellman optimality operator).

$$(\mathcal{B}^\star V)(s)\triangleq\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\Big[R(s,a)+\gamma\sum_{s'}P(s'\mid s,a)\,V(s')\Big].$$

Theorem (contraction). $\|\mathcal{B}^\star V_1-\mathcal{B}^\star V_2\|_\infty\le\gamma\|V_1-V_2\|_\infty$. Consequently $\mathcal{B}^\star$ has a unique fixed point $V^\star$, value iteration converges geometrically,

$$\|V^{(k)}-V^\star\|_\infty\le\gamma^k\,\|V^{(0)}-V^\star\|_\infty,$$

and any greedy policy with respect to $V^\star$ is optimal.

Proof, step 1: a max is 1-Lipschitz

For any two functions $f,g$ on a finite set, $|\max_af(a)-\max_ag(a)|\le\max_a|f(a)-g(a)|$. (If the left maximum is attained at $a_1$, then $\max f-\max g\le f(a_1)-g(a_1)$; argue symmetrically for the other sign.)

Proof, step 2: apply it state by state

Fix $s$ and let $f(a),g(a)$ be the bracketed terms for $V_1$ and $V_2$. The rewards cancel:

$$f(a)-g(a)=\gamma\sum_{s'}P(s'\mid s,a)\big(V_1(s')-V_2(s')\big).$$

Because $\sum_{s'}P(s'\mid s,a)=1$, this is a weighted average of differences, so $|f(a)-g(a)|\le\gamma\|V_1-V_2\|_\infty$. Taking the maximum over $s$ gives the contraction.

Proof, step 3: Banach's fixed-point theorem

$\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}|}$ with the $\infty$-norm is complete, so a $\gamma$-contraction has exactly one fixed point, and iterating from any $V^{(0)}$ approaches it at rate $\gamma^k$. Acting greedily with respect to $V^\star$ attains the maximum in $\mathcal{B}^\star$ at every state, so the greedy policy's value is also a fixed point of $\mathcal{B}^\star$, hence equal to $V^\star$. ∎

1.4Why the forward direction is easy, in four bullets

The four pillars of forward RL
  1. The unknown is finite-dimensional and the equations are exact. $V^\star\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}|}$ solves a finite system.
  2. The solution operator is a contraction. Uniqueness and a geometric convergence rate come for free.
  3. Expectations are finite sums over a known kernel. Every $\sum_{s'}P(s'\mid s,a)(\cdot)$ can be evaluated exactly.
  4. The objective is given. There is a ground truth against which any answer can be scored.

Pillar 4 is what IRL removes, and that is Coordinate 1. Pillars 1–3 are what large-scale or unknown environments remove, and that is Coordinate 2. Forward RL already has a well-developed answer to losing pillars 1–3, which we recall briefly, because IRL inherits it wholesale.

1.5Model-based and model-free forward RL

  1. Model-based. $P$ and $R$ are known. Solve by (1.3), or by value or policy iteration. Value iteration converges linearly with factor $\gamma$; policy iteration terminates after finitely many steps (there are at most $|\mathcal{A}|^{|\mathcal{S}|}$ deterministic policies) and is typically far faster in practice.
  2. Model-free. $P$ is unknown; the agent may only sample transitions. Temporal-difference methods ($Q$-learning, SARSA) replace the exact expectation $\sum_{s'}P(\cdot)$ by sampled successors. Tabular $Q$-learning converges almost surely to $Q^\star$ under the Robbins–Monro conditions $\sum_t\alpha_t=\infty$, $\sum_t\alpha_t^2<\infty$, provided every $(s,a)$ is visited infinitely often [Watkins & Dayan 1992; Tsitsiklis 1994]. With function approximation — policy-gradient methods included [Sutton & Barto 2018] — the combination of bootstrapping, off-policy sampling and approximation, known as the deadly triad, can destroy the contraction, and neither uniqueness nor convergence is guaranteed.
Remark: ill-conditioning as $\gamma\to1$

In the $\infty$-norm, $\kappa_\infty(\mathbf{I}-\gamma\mathbf{P}^\pi)\le\frac{1+\gamma}{1-\gamma}$, so the linear solve degrades as $\gamma\to1^-$. The same $(1-\gamma)^{-1}$ factors reappear in sample-complexity bounds: obtaining an $\varepsilon$-optimal policy with a generative model costs $\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\big(\frac{|\mathcal{S}||\mathcal{A}|}{\varepsilon^2(1-\gamma)^3}\big)$ samples [Azar et al. 2013]. The effective horizon $(1-\gamma)^{-1}$ is the quantity that hurts, in every regime, forward and inverse alike.

Part IIThe ill-posed direction

2Setting up the inverse problem

In many domains — driving, surgery, aerobatic flight, dialogue — writing down a reward is harder than demonstrating the behaviour. Hand-specified rewards are routinely mis-specified, and optimising a mis-specified reward produces the failure mode known as reward hacking. IRL proposes to infer the objective from behaviour instead.

The two directions $$\begin{aligned} &\textbf{Forward RL:}\quad \text{dynamics } \mathcal{M} \;+\; \text{reward } R \;\xrightarrow{\ \text{optimise}\ }\; \text{optimal policy } \pi^\star\\[4pt] &\textbf{Inverse RL:}\quad \text{dynamics } \mathcal{M} \;+\; \text{demonstrations } \mathcal{D}_E \;\xrightarrow{\ \text{infer}\ }\; \text{reward } R \ \text{(then, usually, } \pi) \end{aligned}$$

We observe $m$ trajectories $\mathcal{D}_E=\{\tau^{(1)},\ldots,\tau^{(m)}\}$, with $\tau=(s_0,a_0,s_1,a_1,\ldots,s_T)$, and define the discounted trajectory return $R(\tau)\triangleq\sum_{t\ge0}\gamma^tR(s_t,a_t)$.

Assumption (expert near-optimality). $\mathcal{D}_E$ was generated by a policy $\pi_E$ that is optimal, or near-optimal, for some unknown reward $R^\star$ in the given dynamics $\mathcal{M}$.

This assumption is the behaviour model in its crudest form. Taken literally, it yields only inequalities:

$$J_R(\pi_E)\;\ge\;J_R(\pi)\qquad\text{for all }\pi\in\Pi.$$(2.1)

A system of inequalities is a weak thing to estimate from: it has no notion of “almost satisfied”, and a single noisy demonstration can make it infeasible. Much of the technical content of modern IRL consists of replacing (2.1) by a probabilistic behaviour model, which turns inequalities into a likelihood and therefore into something a gradient can act on. We will watch exactly that happen in Section 5.

3What exactly can be inverted?

Calling IRL “inverse optimisation” invites the wrong intuition, because ordinary inversion presumes injectivity. The forward map

$$R\;\longmapsto\;\Pi^\star(R)=\{\text{policies optimal for }R\}$$(3.1)

is drastically many-to-one. Before choosing an algorithm we should know precisely what information this map destroys.

Three objects that must not be confused
  1. $V^\star$ is unique, given $\mathcal{M}$ and $R$: it is the fixed point of a contraction.
  2. $\pi^\star$ need not be unique: ties in the greedy step give a set $\Pi^\star(R)$.
  3. $R$ is far from unique, given behaviour: $\Pi^\star$ has enormous fibres.

Uniqueness of the forward solution says nothing whatever about uniqueness of the inverse. These three statements are about three different maps.

3.1The solution set is a polyhedral cone

Ng and Russell gave the exact characterisation for a finite MDP and a fixed deterministic policy.

Theorem (consistent rewards; Ng & Russell 2000). Let $\mathcal{S}$ be finite, let $R\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}|}$ be a state-only reward, and let $\mathbf{P}_a$ denote the matrix with entries $P(s'\mid s,a)$. A deterministic policy $\pi$, relabelled so that $\pi(s)\equiv a_1$, is optimal for $R$ if and only if

$$(\mathbf{P}_{a_1}-\mathbf{P}_a)(\mathbf{I}-\gamma\mathbf{P}_{a_1})^{-1}R\;\succeq\;0\qquad\text{for all }a\in\mathcal{A}\setminus\{a_1\}.$$(3.2)

Read (3.2) with Section 1.2 in mind: $(\mathbf{I}-\gamma\mathbf{P}_{a_1})^{-1}R$ is the value vector of $\pi$, and $(\mathbf{P}_{a_1}-\mathbf{P}_a)$ applied to it compares the expected next-state value of the expert's action with that of any deviation. The condition is linear in $R$, which has two immediate consequences.

Corollary (a convex cone containing zero). The set of rewards consistent with an observed policy is a closed convex cone: it is closed under non-negative scaling and under addition. In particular it always contains $R\equiv0$, for which every policy is optimal, so observing behaviour alone can never exclude the degenerate explanation “nothing matters”.

Beyond $R\equiv0$, any constant $R\equiv c$ makes all policies optimal. A subtler and far more important degeneracy is potential-based shaping: for any potential $\Psi:\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}$, the shaped reward

$$R'(s,a,s')=R(s,a)+\gamma\Psi(s')-\Psi(s)$$(3.3)

satisfies $J_{R'}(\pi)=J_R(\pi)-\mathbb{E}_{s_0\sim d_0}[\Psi(s_0)]$ for every $\pi$. The shift is the same for all policies, so the entire preference ordering over policies is preserved and $\Pi^\star(R')=\Pi^\star(R)$. The calculation is a telescoping sum:

Step 1: split the shaped return

By linearity of expectation, the shaped return is the original return plus a shaping term:

$$J_{R'}(\pi)=J_R(\pi)+\mathbb{E}_\pi\Big[\sum_{t\ge0}\big(\gamma^{t+1}\Psi(s_{t+1})-\gamma^t\Psi(s_t)\big)\Big].$$
Step 2: the partial sums telescope

Every intermediate potential appears once with a plus sign and once with a minus sign:

$$\sum_{t=0}^{N-1}\big(\gamma^{t+1}\Psi(s_{t+1})-\gamma^t\Psi(s_t)\big)=\gamma^N\Psi(s_N)-\Psi(s_0).$$
Step 3: take the limit

$\Psi$ is bounded on a finite state space and $\gamma^N\to0$, so the shaping term converges to $-\Psi(s_0)$ along every trajectory. Taking expectations, $J_{R'}(\pi)=J_R(\pi)-\mathbb{E}_{s_0\sim d_0}[\Psi(s_0)]$.

Step 4: the offset does not depend on the policy

The initial distribution $d_0$ is fixed by the dynamics, not chosen by the agent. Every policy is shifted by the same constant, so every comparison $J(\pi_1)\gtrless J(\pi_2)$ is unchanged, and $R$ and $R'$ have exactly the same optimal policies. No amount of behavioural data can tell them apart.

Remark: correct attribution

The optimality-preserving property of potential-based shaping is due to Ng, Harada and Russell (1999), not to the 2000 IRL paper. The 1999 paper also proves the converse: potential-based shaping is the only form of additive reward modification that preserves optimality for all transition kernels. That converse is exactly why potential shaping is the right notion of “nuisance parameter” in IRL, and it is the ambiguity that AIRL sets out to quotient away in Section 11.

3.2A minimal example

Consider a single state and a single decision between left and right; the expert chooses left. All we learn is

$$R(s,\texttt{left})\;\ge\;R(s,\texttt{right}).$$(3.4)

The pairs $(1,0)$, $(10,-3)$ and $(0,-100)$ are equally consistent. One observation cannot pin down two numbers, and repeating the same observation does not help: additional demonstrations add inequalities, which sharpen the preference structure but never fix an absolute scale or offset. Notice, though, that the example is already informative in one respect: it rules out $R(s,\texttt{left})<R(s,\texttt{right})$. IRL is not hopeless; it is partially identifying.

3.3The honest target

Given all this, the scientifically defensible outputs of IRL are:

  1. an equivalence class of rewards inducing the observed behaviour;
  2. a single representative chosen by an explicit selection principle (maximum margin, maximum entropy, a Bayesian prior, sparsity);
  3. or simply a policy whose occupancy measure matches the expert's, with no claim about reward at all.

Item 3 is imitation learning, and it is often what one actually wants. Item 2 is what most IRL papers deliver. Item 1 is what the data actually supports. Confusing 2 with 1 is the single most common overclaim in the literature. Recent work makes the partial-identifiability picture precise and shows that even with infinite data and a known model, the reward is only determined up to explicitly characterisable transformations [Cao et al. 2021; Skalse et al. 2023].

A claim to be suspicious of

“Our method recovers the true reward function.” No method recovers a reward function from behaviour alone. Methods recover a representative of an equivalence class, selected by an assumption the paper made. The useful question is never “is the reward right?” but “is the reward right for the use I have in mind?” The two most common uses — reproducing behaviour in the same MDP, and transferring behaviour to a different MDP — need very different amounts of identification. Section 11 is entirely about the gap between them.

3.4Choosing a reward class

The unknown $R:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}$ is a function; if $\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}$ is continuous it is an infinite-dimensional object. A finite vector $w\in\mathbb{R}^d$ appears only after we choose a model class, most commonly the linear one

$$R_w(s,a)=w^\top\phi(s,a).$$(3.5)

This is a modelling commitment on Coordinate 1, not a law of IRL. Alternatives include kernel and Gaussian-process rewards, neural rewards $R_\theta$, and reward classes defined implicitly by a discriminator architecture. Whether $\phi$ must be hand-designed is taken up in full in Section 7; for now we record what (3.5) buys, because the next two sections lean on it entirely.

Proposition (linearity collapses return into a finite statistic). Under (3.5),

$$J_{R_w}(\pi)=\mathbb{E}_\pi\Big[\sum_{t\ge0}\gamma^tw^\top\phi(s_t,a_t)\Big]=w^\top\,\mathbb{E}_\pi\Big[\sum_{t\ge0}\gamma^t\phi(s_t,a_t)\Big]=w^\top\mu(\pi).$$(3.6)

The vector $\mu(\pi)\in\mathbb{R}^d$ is a sufficient statistic of the policy for the purpose of evaluating any reward in the class: everything else about $\pi$ is irrelevant to $w$. This algebraic fact is what makes classical IRL tractable, and losing it is the price of leaving the linear class.

Part IIITwo selection principles, computed exactly

Throughout this part we stay in the safe corner: $\mathcal{S}$ and $\mathcal{A}$ finite, the kernel $P$ fully known, and $\phi$ fixed in advance. Coordinate 2 therefore costs nothing — every expectation below is a finite sum we can actually evaluate — and we can concentrate wholly on Coordinate 1. This is the only part of the tutorial in which every number can be checked by hand, and it is worth the patience: the objects defined here reappear, in approximated form, in every method that follows.

4Selection principle I: maximum margin

Abbeel and Ng (2004) resolve the ambiguity of Section 3 with a geometric rule: among all rewards consistent with the data, prefer the one under which the expert beats the alternatives by the largest margin.

4.1Feature expectations

Definition (feature expectation).

$$\mu(\pi)\triangleq\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim p_\pi}[\Phi(\tau)]=\mathbb{E}_\pi\Big[\sum_{t\ge0}\gamma^t\phi(s_t,a_t)\Big]\in\mathbb{R}^d,\qquad \mu_E\triangleq\frac1m\sum_{i=1}^m\Phi(\tau^{(i)}).$$

In the safe corner, $\mu(\pi)$ is computed exactly, one feature coordinate at a time: coordinate $j$ is the value function of $\pi$ under the “reward” $\phi_j$, so by (1.3) it costs one linear solve with $\mathbf{I}-\gamma\mathbf{P}^\pi$.

Lemma (feature matching suffices). If $\|\mu(\pi)-\mu_E\|_2\le\varepsilon$, then for every $w$ with $\|w\|_2\le1$,

$$\big|J_{R_w}(\pi)-w^\top\mu_E\big|=\big|w^\top(\mu(\pi)-\mu_E)\big|\le\|w\|_2\,\|\mu(\pi)-\mu_E\|_2\le\varepsilon.$$(4.1)

The equality is (3.6); the first inequality is Cauchy–Schwarz. Matching feature expectations therefore matches expected return simultaneously for every reward in the linear class, without knowing which one is the truth.

This lemma is the reason apprenticeship learning can sidestep identification altogether. It also states its own limitation with unusual clarity: the guarantee is quantified over $w\in\mathbb{R}^d$, and says nothing about rewards outside $\mathrm{span}\{\phi_1,\ldots,\phi_d\}$.

4.2The optimisation

Suppose we already have a collection of policies $\pi_0,\ldots,\pi_{k-1}$ (the algorithm below generates them). Because scaling $w\mapsto\alpha w$ scales any margin, a norm constraint is needed to keep the problem bounded.

Max-margin IRL (Abbeel & Ng, 2004)
$$\begin{aligned}\max_{\xi\in\mathbb{R},\,w\in\mathbb{R}^d}\quad&\xi\\ \text{subject to}\quad&w^\top\mu_E\;\ge\;w^\top\mu(\pi_i)+\xi,\qquad i=0,\ldots,k-1,\\ &\|w\|_2\le1.\end{aligned}$$(4.2)

This is the hard-margin SVM in disguise, with $\mu_E$ the single positive example and the $\mu(\pi_i)$ the negatives. Its dual is a minimum-distance problem,

$$\min_{\bar\mu\in\mathrm{conv}\{\mu(\pi_0),\ldots,\mu(\pi_{k-1})\}}\|\mu_E-\bar\mu\|_2,\qquad\text{with optimal}\quad w\propto\mu_E-\bar\mu,$$(4.3)

which is the geometric content of the algorithm: the reward direction always points from the best current imitation towards the expert. The optimal margin equals the distance from $\mu_E$ to the convex hull; it is positive exactly when the expert's feature expectations lie outside the hull of what we can currently produce. Solving (4.3) directly is Abbeel and Ng's “projection” variant, which avoids a QP solver.

4.3The algorithm, and what it actually returns

The algorithm is a game between two players. A reward player finds the direction $w$ in which the expert is most clearly better than everything produced so far; a policy player then solves the forward problem for that reward, producing a new policy that closes the gap in that direction.

Algorithm 1. Max-margin / apprenticeship learning (Abbeel & Ng, 2004)
  1. Require: dynamics $\mathcal{M}$, features $\phi$, expert data $\mathcal{D}_E$, tolerance $\varepsilon>0$
  2. $\mu_E\gets\frac1m\sum_{i=1}^m\sum_t\gamma^t\phi(s_t^{(i)},a_t^{(i)})$
  3. pick any $\pi_0$; compute $\mu(\pi_0)$ exactly, one linear solve with $\mathbf{I}-\gamma\mathbf{P}^{\pi_0}$ per feature coordinate
  4. for $k=1,2,\ldots$ do
  5. (reward player) solve (4.2) over $\{\mu(\pi_0),\ldots,\mu(\pi_{k-1})\}$ to get $(w^{(k)},\xi^{(k)})$
  6. if $\xi^{(k)}\le\varepsilon$ then break
  7. (policy player) $\pi_k\gets$ exact forward RL solution for $R^{(k)}=(w^{(k)})^\top\phi$
  8. compute $\mu(\pi_k)$ exactly
  9. end for
  10. solve $\lambda^\star=\arg\min_{\lambda\in\Delta_k}\big\|\mu_E-\sum_i\lambda_i\,\mu(\pi_i)\big\|_2$
  11. return the mixed policy $\tilde\pi$: sample $i\sim\lambda^\star$ once at $t=0$, then follow $\pi_i$ forever
The output is a mixture, not a single policy

Algorithm 1 does not return the last $\pi_k$. Its guarantee is for the mixed policy $\tilde\pi$, and the reason is exactly (4.3): what is provably close to $\mu_E$ is a point in the convex hull of the $\mu(\pi_i)$, and convex combinations of feature expectations are realised by randomising over policies, not by any single one of them. This works because occupancy measures mix linearly, $\rho_{\tilde\pi}=\sum_i\lambda_i\rho_{\pi_i}$, a fact we exploit again in Section 9. Presentations that return “the final policy” have quietly dropped the theorem.

Theorem (convergence, informal). If $\phi:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to[0,1]^d$, then $\|\mu(\pi)\|_2\le\sqrt d/(1-\gamma)$ for all $\pi$, and Algorithm 1 produces $\|\mu(\tilde\pi)-\mu_E\|_2\le\varepsilon$ after

$$k=O\Big(\frac{d}{(1-\gamma)^2\varepsilon^2}\log\frac{d}{(1-\gamma)\varepsilon}\Big)$$

iterations — notably, a bound independent of $|\mathcal{S}|$. Combined with (4.1), $\tilde\pi$ then performs within $\varepsilon$ of the expert under every reward in the class.

4.4Limitations, stated precisely

  1. Ties are broken arbitrarily. Many $(w,\xi)$ may achieve the same margin; the QP picks one. The selection principle is a convenience, not a discovery.
  2. Sub-optimal experts break it. The constraints assert that the expert is best. A noisy expert makes the feasible set shrink, or become empty, and there is no likelihood to absorb the noise. This is precisely the gap maximum entropy fills.
  3. The inner solve is a full forward RL problem per iteration. In the safe corner it is exact; outside it, it is the bottleneck.
  4. It is silent about which reward is right. Its guarantee is about performance, quantified over the whole class.
Ledger · Max-margin IRL

The expert is exactly optimal; the reward is linear in a fixed $\phi$; ties are broken by the maximal margin.
Approximated (Coordinate 2)

5Selection principle II: maximum entropy

Max-margin IRL treats the expert as infallible. Real demonstrations are noisy — and worse, systematically noisy: an expert who is indifferent between two routes will take each about half the time, and a method built on strict inequalities has nothing to say about that. Ziebart et al. (2008) replace the inequality model by a probabilistic one, and then select among the surviving models by maximum entropy, in the spirit of Jaynes's original principle (1957).

The setting for this section

Throughout Section 5 we take finite $\mathcal{S}$ and $\mathcal{A}$, a finite horizon $T$, a known kernel $P$, and — following the original paper — we set $\gamma=1$, writing $\Phi(\tau)=\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\phi(s_t,a_t)$. Discounting and infinite horizons are restored in Section 6, where we explain why they are genuinely more delicate than simply inserting $\gamma^t$.

5.1Why a distribution over trajectories, and over what?

We want a model $p(\tau)$ that (i) reproduces the expert's feature statistics and (ii) commits to nothing else. The second requirement is what “maximum entropy” formalises. But entropy of what, exactly? This is where most treatments go wrong, so we go slowly.

A trajectory is produced by two agencies: the learner, who chooses actions, and the environment, which chooses successor states. If we maximise the plain Shannon entropy $-\sum_\tau p(\tau)\log p(\tau)$, we implicitly declare every trajectory equally plausible a priori, including trajectories the environment would essentially never produce. That is obviously wrong. The fix is to measure entropy relative to a reference measure that encodes what the environment does.

Definition (dynamics-induced reference measure). For $\tau=(s_0,a_0,\ldots,s_T)$,

$$q(\tau)\triangleq d_0(s_0)\prod_{t=0}^{T-1}P(s_{t+1}\mid s_t,a_t).$$(5.1)
Remark: $q$ is a measure, not a probability distribution

Summing over $\mathcal{T}$ gives $\sum_\tau q(\tau)=|\mathcal{A}|^T$, because $q$ integrates out the environment but not the actions. It is exactly the prior that is “uniform over your own choices, honest about nature's”. Nothing below requires $q$ to be normalised.

Definition (relative entropy objective).

$$\mathcal{H}_q(p)\triangleq-\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}}p(\tau)\log\frac{p(\tau)}{q(\tau)}=-\mathrm{KL}(p\,\|\,q).$$(5.2)

When $P$ is deterministic and $d_0$ is uniform, $q$ is constant on feasible trajectories and $\mathcal{H}_q$ reduces to Shannon entropy up to an additive constant. That special case is the one usually presented; it is not the general case.

Two errors this definition prevents

(1) The retrofit error. If one maximises plain Shannon entropy, the stationary solution is $p(\tau)\propto e^{w^\top\Phi(\tau)}$ with no dynamics factor. Many expositions derive that, and then later assert without comment that “really” $p(\tau)\propto q(\tau)e^{w^\top\Phi(\tau)}$. These are solutions to two different programs. Putting $q$ into the objective from the first line makes the derivation and the final formula agree.

(2) The continuum error. If one starts with $-\int p\log p\,d\tau$ over a continuous trajectory space, the objective is a differential entropy: it is not invariant under reparameterisation of $\tau$ and it is not bounded above. The relative entropy $-\mathrm{KL}(p\|q)$ has neither defect. This is a further reason to begin in the discrete setting: the pathology is invisible until you try to make things rigorous.

5.2The primal program

$$\begin{aligned}\max_p\quad&\mathcal{H}_q(p)=-\sum_\tau p(\tau)\log\frac{p(\tau)}{q(\tau)}\\ \text{s.t.}\quad&\sum_\tau p(\tau)\,\Phi(\tau)=\mu_E\qquad\text{(feature matching)}\\ &\sum_\tau p(\tau)=1,\quad p(\tau)\ge0\qquad\text{(normalisation)}\end{aligned}$$(5.3)

The feasible set is the intersection of the probability simplex with $d$ affine constraints, and $\mathcal{H}_q$ is strictly concave on it, so the solution — if the feasible set is non-empty — is unique. Note that this uniqueness is over distributions, not over rewards; the non-identifiability of Section 3 still applies.

5.3Variational derivation

This is the central derivation of maximum-entropy IRL. We split it into four steps, each doing exactly one thing.

Step 1: write down the Lagrangian

Introduce a multiplier $w\in\mathbb{R}^d$ for feature matching and $c\in\mathbb{R}$ for normalisation:

$$\mathcal{L}(p,w,c)=-\sum_\tau p(\tau)\log\frac{p(\tau)}{q(\tau)}+w^\top\Big(\sum_\tau p(\tau)\Phi(\tau)-\mu_E\Big)+c\Big(\sum_\tau p(\tau)-1\Big).$$(5.4)

The three terms are the relative-entropy objective, the “features equal $\mu_E$” constraint and the “probabilities sum to one” constraint. Non-negativity will turn out to hold automatically.

Step 2: stationarity in $p(\tau)$

Fix one trajectory $\tau$ and treat $\mathcal{L}$ as a function of the single number $p(\tau)$. Using $\frac{\partial}{\partial p}\big[-p\log(p/q)\big]=-\log\frac pq-1$,

$$\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial p(\tau)}=-\log\frac{p(\tau)}{q(\tau)}-1+w^\top\Phi(\tau)+c=0.$$(5.5)

Because the problem is concave in $p$ with affine constraints, a stationary point of the Lagrangian is the optimum.

Step 3: solve for the form of $p(\tau)$

Rearrange (5.5) and exponentiate:

$$\log\frac{p(\tau)}{q(\tau)}=w^\top\Phi(\tau)+c-1\quad\Longrightarrow\quad p(\tau)=q(\tau)\exp\big(w^\top\Phi(\tau)+c-1\big).$$(5.6)

The structure is already visible: a dynamics factor $q(\tau)$ times the exponential of the reward. The result is automatically positive wherever $q$ is. All that remains is to pin down the constant $c$.

Step 4: normalise, and meet the partition function

Imposing $\sum_\tau p(\tau)=1$ gives $e^{c-1}\sum_\tau q(\tau)e^{w^\top\Phi(\tau)}=1$, that is, $e^{c-1}=1/Z(w)$ with the partition function

$$Z(w)\triangleq\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}}q(\tau)\,e^{w^\top\Phi(\tau)}.$$(5.7)
The maximum-entropy trajectory model
$$p_w(\tau)=\frac{1}{Z(w)}\;\underbrace{q(\tau)}_{\text{what nature does}}\;\underbrace{e^{w^\top\Phi(\tau)}}_{\text{what the agent wants}},\qquad Z(w)=\sum_\tau q(\tau)\,e^{w^\top\Phi(\tau)}.$$(5.8)

Two trajectories with equal reward need not be equally likely: the one the environment resists is rarer. Exponentially better trajectories are exponentially more likely, but never certain — which is precisely the graceful handling of a sub-optimal expert that max-margin lacked.

5.4The dual is maximum likelihood, and it is convex

Having found the form of the optimal $p$, we substitute it back to obtain a problem in $w$ alone.

Step 1: evaluate the log-ratio at the optimum

From (5.8), $\log p_w(\tau)-\log q(\tau)=w^\top\Phi(\tau)-\log Z(w)$.

Step 2: substitute into the Lagrangian

The entropy term becomes $-\sum_\tau p_w(\tau)\big(w^\top\Phi(\tau)-\log Z(w)\big)=-w^\top\sum_\tau p_w\Phi+\log Z(w)$. The normalisation term vanishes because $p_w$ sums to one, and the feature term contributes $+w^\top\sum_\tau p_w\Phi-w^\top\mu_E$, which cancels the first piece.

Step 3: the dual objective

What survives is the dual function, to be minimised over $w$:

$$g(w)\triangleq\log Z(w)-w^\top\mu_E.$$(5.9)

Theorem (duality and maximum likelihood). Minimising (5.9) is exactly maximising the average log-likelihood of the demonstrations under $p_w$:

$$\frac1m\sum_{i=1}^m\log p_w(\tau^{(i)})=\frac1m\sum_{i=1}^m\Big[\log q(\tau^{(i)})+w^\top\Phi(\tau^{(i)})-\log Z(w)\Big]=\underbrace{w^\top\mu_E-\log Z(w)}_{=\,-g(w)}+\underbrace{\tfrac1m\textstyle\sum_i\log q(\tau^{(i)})}_{\text{does not involve }w}.$$(5.10)

The dropped term is the log-likelihood of the observed transitions under the true dynamics. It is a constant of the optimisation, which is why it is usually invisible.

Theorem (convexity). $w\mapsto\log Z(w)$ is convex, with

$$\nabla_w\log Z(w)=\mathbb{E}_{p_w}[\Phi(\tau)],\qquad\nabla_w^2\log Z(w)=\mathrm{Cov}_{p_w}[\Phi(\tau)]\succeq0.$$(5.11)

Hence $g$ is convex. It is strictly convex, and the optimal $w$ unique, precisely when the features $\Phi$ are not almost surely confined to a proper affine subspace.

Proof, step 1: the gradient

$\nabla_wZ=\sum_\tau q(\tau)e^{w^\top\Phi(\tau)}\Phi(\tau)$, so dividing by $Z$ gives $\nabla_w\log Z=\sum_\tau p_w(\tau)\Phi(\tau)=\mathbb{E}_{p_w}[\Phi]$.

Proof, step 2: the Hessian

Differentiate $\sum_\tau p_w(\tau)\Phi(\tau)$ once more, using $\nabla_wp_w(\tau)=p_w(\tau)\big(\Phi(\tau)-\mathbb{E}_{p_w}[\Phi]\big)$:

$$\nabla_w^2\log Z=\mathbb{E}_{p_w}[\Phi\Phi^\top]-\mathbb{E}_{p_w}[\Phi]\,\mathbb{E}_{p_w}[\Phi]^\top=\mathrm{Cov}_{p_w}[\Phi].$$

A covariance matrix is positive semi-definite, and it is positive definite unless some non-zero direction $v$ has $v^\top\Phi$ constant under $p_w$. ∎

Corollary: the learning rule
$$\boxed{\;-\nabla_wg(w)=\mu_E-\mathbb{E}_{p_w}[\Phi(\tau)]\;}$$(5.12)

Gradient ascent moves $w$ so as to close the gap between what the expert did and what the current model predicts. At the optimum the two feature expectations coincide, so the maximum likelihood solution satisfies the feature-matching constraint of (5.3) exactly.

Remark: a pleasing tension worth noticing

The convexity theorem says the reward parameter $w$ has a unique optimum. Section 3 says the reward function is not identified. Both are true. The resolution is that maximum entropy is a selection principle: it picks one point out of the equivalence class, and picks it uniquely. Uniqueness of an estimator is not identifiability of an estimand.

5.5Computing $Z$: the whole difficulty, made concrete

The learning rule (5.12) looks like a two-line algorithm, and the entire remainder of this tutorial exists because of the innocuous term $\mathbb{E}_{p_w}[\Phi]$. Evaluating it requires $Z(w)$, and $Z(w)$ is a sum over $\mathcal{T}$. Write $R=R_w$ for brevity.

Step 1: enumeration is hopeless, but finite

With $|\mathcal{A}|$ actions and horizon $T$, $|\mathcal{T}|$ grows like $|\mathcal{A}|^T$ (times the branching of $P$). For a $5\times5$ gridworld with 4 actions and $T=20$ that is $4^{20}\approx10^{12}$ trajectories: finite, and still impossible. The value of the discrete formulation is not that we enumerate; it is that $Z$ is unambiguously defined, so we can look for structure.

Step 2: the structure is that $q$ factorises

Both ingredients of the summand are products or sums over time: $q(\tau)=d_0(s_0)\prod_tP(s_{t+1}\mid s_t,a_t)$ and $\Phi(\tau)=\sum_t\phi(s_t,a_t)$, so $e^{w^\top\Phi(\tau)}=\prod_te^{R(s_t,a_t)}$. The summand is therefore a product of per-step factors, and the sum over trajectories can be pushed inside, one time step at a time, from the end backwards.

Step 3: the backward recursion

Let $Z_t(s)$ be the sum, over all suffixes $(a_t,s_{t+1},\ldots,s_T)$ started at $s_t=s$, of $\prod_{u\ge t}P(s_{u+1}\mid s_u,a_u)\,e^{R(s_u,a_u)}$. Splitting off the first action and successor gives

$$Z_T(s)\triangleq1,\qquad Z_t(s)=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}e^{R(s,a)}\sum_{s'\in\mathcal{S}}P(s'\mid s,a)\,Z_{t+1}(s'),\quad t=T-1,\ldots,0,$$(5.13)

and averaging over the start state gives $Z(w)=\sum_sd_0(s)Z_0(s)$.

Step 4: the same recursion in log space

Writing $V_t(s)=\log Z_t(s)$ turns products into sums and exposes a soft Bellman backup:

$$V_t(s)=\log\sum_a\exp\Big(\underbrace{R(s,a)+\log\sum_{s'}P(s'\mid s,a)\,e^{V_{t+1}(s')}}_{=:\,Q_t(s,a)}\Big).$$(5.14)

The cost is $O(T|\mathcal{S}|^2|\mathcal{A}|)$ instead of enumerating all $O(|\mathcal{A}|^T)$ action sequences. Note that $Q_t(s,a)$ denotes the whole argument of the exponential, immediate reward included.

Answer: where does knowledge of $P$ enter? (first place)

Look at (5.13). The kernel $P(s'\mid s,a)$ is consumed inside the inner sum, once per $(s,a)$ per time step. This is the first of exactly two places where MaxEnt IRL needs a model. The second appears in Section 5.8. When we ask, in Section 8, “what if $P$ is unknown?”, these two sums are the entire content of the question.

5.7The stochastic-dynamics trap, and maximum causal entropy

The worked example was deterministic, and that was not laziness. Look again at (5.14). The inner term

$$\log\sum_{s'}P(s'\mid s,a)\,e^{V_{t+1}(s')}$$(5.15)

is a soft maximum over successor states, not an expectation. Since $\log\mathbb{E}[e^X]\ge\mathbb{E}[X]$ by Jensen's inequality, it systematically over-values actions with high variance in their outcomes. The pathology is sharper than a bias in a formula: it is a statement about what the model believes.

Proposition (the naive model secretly rewrites the dynamics). Under $p_w$ of (5.8), the conditional law of the successor state is

$$p_w(s_{t+1}=s'\mid s_t=s,a_t=a)=\frac{P(s'\mid s,a)\,e^{V_{t+1}(s')}}{\sum_{s''}P(s''\mid s,a)\,e^{V_{t+1}(s'')}}\;\ne\;P(s'\mid s,a).$$(5.16)

The inequality holds in general: equality requires $V_{t+1}$ to be constant on the support of $P(\cdot\mid s,a)$.

Proof. Marginalise (5.8) over all suffixes after $s_{t+1}$ using the backward recursion; the suffix sum contributes the factor $Z_{t+1}(s')=e^{V_{t+1}(s')}$, which multiplies $P(s'\mid s,a)$ before renormalisation. ∎

A two-line illustration

From some state, action gamble leads with probability $\frac12$ each to a state worth $V_{t+1}=10$ or to one worth $0$; action safe leads for certain to a state worth $5$ (both actions have zero immediate reward). Their expected values are identical, $5$ and $5$. But the naive backup scores gamble at $\log\big(\tfrac12e^{10}+\tfrac12e^{0}\big)=9.307$ against $5$ for safe, so the model prefers gambling by a factor $e^{4.307}\approx74$. By (5.16) it also believes the gamble pays off with probability $e^{10}/(e^{10}+1)\approx0.99995$ rather than $0.5$.

What this means

The maximum-entropy trajectory model with stochastic dynamics is not a model of an agent choosing actions in the given MDP. It is a model of an agent who also tilts nature towards fortunate outcomes. Fitted to a real expert, it will explain lucky outcomes as intentional and infer rewards that reward luck. In a deterministic MDP the tilt is vacuous ($P$ is a point mass), which is why the original presentation is correct as stated — and why the problem is so easy to miss.

The remedy is to stop optimising over arbitrary trajectory distributions and to optimise over policies instead, forcing the true dynamics to appear as an unchangeable factor.

Definition (causal entropy). For a (possibly time-dependent) policy $\pi$, let $p_\pi(\tau)=d_0(s_0)\prod_t\pi_t(a_t\mid s_t)P(s_{t+1}\mid s_t,a_t)$ and

$$\mathcal{H}^{\mathrm{causal}}(\pi)\triangleq\mathbb{E}_{p_\pi}\Big[\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}-\log\pi_t(a_t\mid s_t)\Big].$$(5.17)

Only the agent's own choices are counted as uncertainty; randomness supplied by nature is not credited to the agent.

Theorem (maximum causal entropy IRL; Ziebart 2010). The solution of

$$\max_\pi\;\mathcal{H}^{\mathrm{causal}}(\pi)\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\mathbb{E}_{p_\pi}[\Phi(\tau)]=\mu_E$$(5.18)

is the soft-optimal policy for $R_w=w^\top\phi$, with $w$ the multiplier, given by the backward recursion (with $V_T\equiv0$)

$$\begin{aligned}Q_t(s,a)&=R_w(s,a)+\sum_{s'}P(s'\mid s,a)\,V_{t+1}(s'),\\ V_t(s)&=\log\sum_a\exp Q_t(s,a),\\ \pi_t(a\mid s)&=\exp\big(Q_t(s,a)-V_t(s)\big).\end{aligned}$$(5.19)

The dual objective remains convex and its gradient remains $\mu_E-\mathbb{E}_{p_\pi}[\Phi]$.

The one-symbol difference

Compare (5.14) with (5.19). Naive MaxEnt takes a soft maximum over both actions and successor states; maximum causal entropy takes a soft maximum over actions and a plain expectation over successor states:

$$\text{naive:}\ \ \log\sum_a\exp\Big(R+\log\sum_{s'}P\,e^{V}\Big)\qquad\qquad\text{causal:}\ \ \log\sum_a\exp\Big(R+\sum_{s'}P\,V\Big)$$

They coincide exactly when $P$ is deterministic. In the gamble illustration above, the causal backup scores both actions at $5$ and is correctly indifferent. Every modern method — soft $Q$-learning, soft actor–critic, GAIL's entropy term, AIRL — uses the causal version. When a paper says “MaxEnt IRL”, it almost always means this.

5.8The forward pass: from a policy to expected features

The backward pass produced a policy. We still need $\mathbb{E}_{p_\pi}[\Phi]$, which requires knowing where that policy goes. That is a forward sweep.

Proposition (forward state-visitation recursion). Let $d_t(s)=\Pr_{p_\pi}(s_t=s)$. Then $d_0$ is the initial distribution and

$$d_{t+1}(s')=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}d_t(s)\,\pi_t(a\mid s)\,P(s'\mid s,a),$$(5.20)

whence

$$\mathbb{E}_{p_\pi}[\Phi]=\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\sum_{s,a}d_t(s)\,\pi_t(a\mid s)\,\phi(s,a).$$(5.21)
Answer: where does knowledge of $P$ enter? (second and last place)

Equation (5.20). Together with the successor expectation in (5.19), these are the only two consumers of $P$ in exact MaxEnt IRL. A backward pass computes what the agent would do; a forward pass computes where doing it takes them. Both need the kernel; neither needs anything else.

5.9Occupancy measures: the same object, better named

Equation (5.21) is a weighted average of $\phi$ over state–action pairs. Naming that weighting will pay for itself many times over.

Definition (discounted occupancy measure).

$$\rho_\pi(s,a)\triangleq(1-\gamma)\sum_{t=0}^\infty\gamma^t\Pr_\pi(s_t=s,a_t=a),$$(5.22)

a probability distribution over $\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}$ (the factor $1-\gamma$ makes the weights $\gamma^t$ sum to one). Then

$$\mu(\pi)=\frac1{1-\gamma}\sum_{s,a}\rho_\pi(s,a)\,\phi(s,a),\qquad J_R(\pi)=\frac1{1-\gamma}\sum_{s,a}\rho_\pi(s,a)\,R(s,a).$$(5.23)
Remark: occupancy is not action frequency

$\rho_\pi$ is a distribution over pairs. “Brake” in front of a pedestrian and “brake” on an empty motorway are different events, because their states differ. MaxEnt IRL never merely counts action labels.

Equation (5.23) says that return is a linear functional of occupancy. All of Part V is built on that sentence.

5.10The complete exact algorithm

Because the objective is convex, any diminishing-step gradient scheme converges to the global optimum in $w$. The per-iteration cost is $O(T|\mathcal{S}|^2|\mathcal{A}|)$ for each pass — linear in the horizon, quadratic in the number of states. That quadratic is the thing that will not survive contact with a real problem.

Algorithm 2. Exact maximum causal entropy IRL (finite $\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A}$, known $P$)
  1. Require: dynamics $\mathcal{M}$ with known $P$, features $\phi$, expert data $\mathcal{D}_E$, step size $\eta$
  2. $\mu_E\gets\frac1m\sum_i\Phi(\tau^{(i)})$; initialise $w\gets0$
  3. for $k=1,2,\ldots$ until $\|\mu_E-\hat\mu\|$ is small do
  4. $R_w(s,a)\gets w^\top\phi(s,a)$ for all $(s,a)$
  5. backward pass: $V_T\equiv0$; for $t=T-1,\ldots,0$ compute $Q_t,V_t,\pi_t$ by (5.19) ▷ uses $P$
  6. forward pass: start from $d_0$; for $t=0,\ldots,T-1$ propagate $d_{t+1}$ by (5.20) ▷ uses $P$
  7. $\hat\mu\gets\sum_{t,s,a}d_t(s)\,\pi_t(a\mid s)\,\phi(s,a)$
  8. $w\gets w+\eta\,(\mu_E-\hat\mu)$ ▷ the learning rule (5.12)
  9. end for
  10. return $w$ and the soft-optimal policy $\pi$

Part IVThree exits from the safe corner

Algorithm 2 is a complete, correct, globally convergent IRL method. It is also unusable on any problem anyone actually cares about, for three separate reasons. Each reason is one of the assumptions we made on entering Part III, and each is a question every reader of the IRL literature ends up asking. We take them one at a time, and each time we ask the same two things: what survives, and what must be replaced?

6Exit A: what changes when the problem is not finite?

“Not finite” hides three separate difficulties: an unbounded horizon, an unbounded number of states, and a continuum of actions. They fail differently.

6.1Infinite horizon: discounting, and why trajectory entropy dies

The finite-horizon derivation of Section 5 does not survive $T\to\infty$ as written, and the reason is not the return but the entropy. The causal entropy $\mathbb{E}\big[\sum_{t<T}-\log\pi(a_t\mid s_t)\big]$ grows linearly in $T$ for any non-deterministic policy, so the objective diverges. The repair is the same discounting we already apply to reward.

Definition (discounted causal entropy and the soft objective).

$$\mathcal{H}_\gamma(\pi)\triangleq\mathbb{E}_\pi\Big[\sum_{t=0}^\infty\gamma^t\big(-\log\pi(a_t\mid s_t)\big)\Big],\qquad J_R^\beta(\pi)\triangleq J_R(\pi)+\beta\,\mathcal{H}_\gamma(\pi).$$(6.1)

The temperature $\beta>0$ is now explicit; Section 5 used $\beta=1$.

Definition (soft Bellman operator).

$$(\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{soft}}Q)(s,a)\triangleq R(s,a)+\gamma\,\mathbb{E}_{s'\sim P(\cdot\mid s,a)}\Big[\beta\log\sum_{a'}\exp\big(Q(s',a')/\beta\big)\Big].$$(6.2)

Note that successors enter through a plain expectation: this is the discounted, stationary version of the causal backup (5.19), not of the naive one. Whether it still has a unique fixed point rests on one small lemma.

Lemma (log-sum-exp is a non-expansion). For $x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{A}|}$ and $\beta>0$,

$$\Big|\beta\log\sum_ae^{x_a/\beta}-\beta\log\sum_ae^{y_a/\beta}\Big|\le\|x-y\|_\infty.$$
Proof, step 1: a uniform bound

Let $\delta=\|x-y\|_\infty$. Then $x_a\le y_a+\delta$ for every $a$.

Proof, step 2: exponentiate and sum

The exponential is increasing, so $e^{x_a/\beta}\le e^{\delta/\beta}e^{y_a/\beta}$ for every $a$, and summing,

$$\sum_ae^{x_a/\beta}\le e^{\delta/\beta}\sum_ae^{y_a/\beta}.$$
Proof, step 3: take $\beta\log$ of both sides

$\beta\log$ is increasing and turns the factor $e^{\delta/\beta}$ into an additive $\delta$:

$$\beta\log\sum_ae^{x_a/\beta}\le\beta\log\sum_ae^{y_a/\beta}+\delta.$$

Exchanging the roles of $x$ and $y$ gives the reverse inequality, and together they bound the absolute difference by $\delta$. ∎

Theorem (soft value iteration converges). $\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{soft}}$ is a $\gamma$-contraction in $\|\cdot\|_\infty$. It therefore has a unique fixed point $Q_{\mathrm{soft}}$, soft value iteration converges geometrically, and the soft-optimal policy

$$\pi_{\mathrm{soft}}(a\mid s)\propto\exp\big(Q_{\mathrm{soft}}(s,a)/\beta\big)$$

maximises the soft objective (6.1).

Proof. For two $Q$-functions, the rewards cancel in $\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{soft}}Q_1-\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{soft}}Q_2$. Apply the lemma pointwise inside the expectation in (6.2), with $x=Q_1(s',\cdot)$ and $y=Q_2(s',\cdot)$, and use $\sum_{s'}P(s'\mid s,a)=1$, exactly as in the hard contraction proof of Section 1.3. ∎

Remark: the temperature interpolates

Since

$$\max_aQ(s,a)\;\le\;\beta\log\sum_ae^{Q(s,a)/\beta}\;\le\;\max_aQ(s,a)+\beta\log|\mathcal{A}|,$$

the soft operator is within $\beta\log|\mathcal{A}|$ of $\mathcal{B}^\star$ everywhere. As $\beta\to0$ we recover hard optimality and max-margin's behaviour model; as $\beta\to\infty$ the policy becomes uniform and the demonstrations are treated as uninformative. The temperature is a modelling choice on Coordinate 1: it says how rational you believe the expert to be. Fitting $\beta$ from data is possible but is entangled with the scale of $R$, since $(R,\beta)$ and $(cR,c\beta)$ give the same policy — one more instance of the identifiability theme.

What survived Exit A, part 1

The whole structure of Section 5 survives an infinite horizon, provided we use discounted causal entropy and a stationary soft Bellman fixed point in place of a finite backward sweep. What we lose is only the interpretation of $\log Z$ as the log-partition function of a finite trajectory set; a soft value function takes its place. Nothing statistical changes.

6.2Large or continuous state spaces

Here the loss is real. Both passes of Algorithm 2 are tabular sweeps costing $O(|\mathcal{S}|^2|\mathcal{A}|)$ per step. Three things break at once.

  1. The tables do not fit. $V$, $\pi$ and $d_t$ are arrays indexed by $\mathcal{S}$. For continuous $\mathcal{S}$ they are functions and must be approximated — by linear architectures or, in practice, neural networks. This reintroduces every hazard of the deadly triad into the inner loop of IRL.
  2. Discretisation does not save you. An $\varepsilon$-net of $[0,1]^n$ has $\varepsilon^{-n}$ cells. A ten-dimensional state at resolution $0.1$ is $10^{10}$ cells, and the sweep is quadratic in that.
  3. Sums become integrals, and the reference measure becomes load-bearing. In (5.7), $\sum_\tau q(\tau)(\cdot)$ becomes $\int(\cdot)\,dq(\tau)$. This is precisely where the relative-entropy formulation of Section 5.1 pays off: $-\mathrm{KL}(p\|q)$ is well defined and reparameterisation-invariant on a continuous space, whereas $-\int p\log p$ is neither. Had we begun with the differential entropy, the objective would change value under a smooth change of coordinates on $\mathcal{T}$ — an absurdity for a principle that is supposed to encode ignorance.

6.3Continuous actions

The soft maximum over actions, $\beta\log\sum_a\exp(Q/\beta)$, becomes $\beta\log\int_{\mathcal{A}}\exp\big(Q(s,a)/\beta\big)\,da$: an integral with no closed form for a general $Q$. The standard resolution is variational: represent the policy explicitly and fit it to the Boltzmann target,

$$\pi\;\leftarrow\;\arg\min_{\pi'\in\Pi_\theta}\;\mathrm{KL}\Big(\pi'(\cdot\mid s)\;\Big\|\;\tfrac{1}{Z(s)}\exp\big(Q(s,\cdot)/\beta\big)\Big),$$(6.3)

which is exactly the policy-improvement step of soft actor–critic [Haarnoja et al. 2018], and an instance of the broader control-as-inference picture in which entropy-regularised RL is exact probabilistic inference in a graphical model [Levine 2018]. Note the structure: an intractable normaliser over actions is replaced by a learned sampler. We are about to meet the same move one level up, where an intractable normaliser over trajectories is replaced by a learned policy.

7Exit B: must the features be hand-crafted?

Short answer: no, and modern methods do not hand-craft them. But the question hides a more useful one — what was the fixed $\phi$ doing for us? — and the honest answer is: quite a lot.

7.1What a fixed linear class buys

  1. A sufficient statistic. By the proposition of Section 3.4, a policy is summarised for all reward-evaluation purposes by $\mu(\pi)\in\mathbb{R}^d$. An infinite-dimensional object collapses to $d$ numbers.
  2. Convexity. The convexity theorem of Section 5.4 needs $R$ to be linear in the parameter. This is what makes MaxEnt IRL a well-behaved convex estimation problem.
  3. A class-wide guarantee. The feature-matching lemma (4.1) certifies performance for every reward in the class at once, which is much stronger than certifying it for one estimate.
  4. Interpretability. “The expert weights collision risk eight times as heavily as lane deviation” is a sentence one can act on.

7.2What it costs

Misspecification is invisible from inside

Every guarantee above is quantified over $w\in\mathbb{R}^d$ with $\phi$ fixed. If the true reward is not in $\mathrm{span}\{\phi_1,\ldots,\phi_d\}$, matching $\mu_E$ implies nothing about $J_{R^\star}$. Worse, the fit will look fine: the residual lives in a direction the model cannot see. Classical apprenticeship-learning results on driving worked because a human had already encoded the task into features such as “distance to the nearest car” and “off-road indicator”. The domain knowledge was in $\phi$, not in the algorithm.

7.3The ambiguity that appears when $\phi$ is learned

Suppose we learn $\phi_\eta$ and $w$ jointly. For any invertible $A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$,

$$w^\top\phi(s,a)=\big(A^{-\top}w\big)^\top\big(A\,\phi(s,a)\big),$$(7.1)

so $(w,\phi)$ and $(A^{-\top}w,A\phi)$ are indistinguishable. Only the scalar field $R$ and the span of the features are meaningful; individual coordinates of $w$ are not. Statements such as “the model learned that feature 3 matters” are therefore meaningless without a convention that pins down $A$. This is a Coordinate-1 ambiguity added by learning features, on top of the reward ambiguity we already had.

7.4The good news: the learning rule does not change

One might fear that abandoning linearity forces a new derivation. It does not.

Proposition (MaxEnt gradient for an arbitrary reward class). Let $R_\theta$ be any differentiable reward parameterisation, and let $R_\theta(\tau)=\sum_t\gamma^tR_\theta(s_t,a_t)$. With $p_\theta(\tau)\propto q(\tau)e^{R_\theta(\tau)}$, the log-likelihood $L(\theta)=\frac1m\sum_iR_\theta(\tau^{(i)})-\log Z(\theta)$ (dropping the $\theta$-free $\log q$ term, as in (5.10)) has gradient

$$\nabla_\theta L(\theta)=\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\mathcal{D}_E}\big[\nabla_\theta R_\theta(\tau)\big]}_{\text{expert}}-\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim p_\theta}\big[\nabla_\theta R_\theta(\tau)\big]}_{\text{current model}}.$$(7.2)
Step 1: the expert term is immediate

The gradient of $\frac1m\sum_iR_\theta(\tau^{(i)})$ is the empirical average $\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_E}[\nabla_\theta R_\theta]$. Everything interesting is in $\nabla_\theta\log Z(\theta)$.

Step 2: differentiate under the sum
$$\nabla_\theta\log Z=\frac1Z\,\nabla_\theta\sum_\tau q(\tau)e^{R_\theta(\tau)}=\frac1Z\sum_\tau q(\tau)e^{R_\theta(\tau)}\,\nabla_\theta R_\theta(\tau).$$

$q(\tau)$ does not depend on $\theta$, so it passes straight through; the chain rule on the exponential pulls out $\nabla_\theta R_\theta$.

Step 3: recognise an expectation under the model

The weights $q(\tau)e^{R_\theta(\tau)}/Z$ are exactly $p_\theta(\tau)$, so

$$\nabla_\theta\log Z(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim p_\theta}\big[\nabla_\theta R_\theta(\tau)\big].$$

Subtracting gives (7.2). In the linear case $R_w=w^\top\phi$ we have $\nabla_wR_w(\tau)=\Phi(\tau)$, and (7.2) reduces exactly to the learning rule (5.12). ∎

Equation (7.2) is (5.12) with $\Phi$ replaced by $\nabla_\theta R_\theta$. Feature matching was never the principle; matching the sufficient statistics of the reward class was. This observation is what makes deep MaxEnt IRL [Wulfmeier et al. 2015] and guided cost learning [Finn et al. 2016] possible with no new theory.

7.5What is lost: convexity, and the need for inductive bias

With $R_\theta$ nonlinear, $L$ is no longer concave. We get local optima, sensitivity to initialisation, and — most importantly — the capacity to overfit. A sufficiently expressive $R_\theta$ can memorise the demonstrations: assign huge reward exactly on the observed state–action pairs and zero elsewhere. That reward explains the data perfectly and is useless.

Useful counter-pressures, all of which are Coordinate-1 commitments:

  • Architectural restriction. Most powerfully, making $R$ depend on the state only, $R_\theta(s)$. We will see in Section 11 that this is not a convenience but a necessary condition for a transfer guarantee.
  • Smoothness and norm penalties on $R_\theta$; gradient penalties on a discriminator.
  • Bottlenecks, including explicit information bottlenecks on the reward representation.
  • Multi-task structure: several tasks sharing one representation with different weights, which restores identifiability of the shared part.
  • Priors, i.e. Bayesian IRL [Ramachandran & Amir 2007], which makes the selection principle explicit rather than implicit.
Answer to Exit B

No, $\phi$ need not be hand-crafted, and the maximum-entropy learning rule (7.2) is unchanged when it is not. What changes is that the estimation problem stops being convex and starts requiring regularisation, and that a new reparameterisation ambiguity is introduced. The choice of reward class does not disappear when features are learned — it becomes the choice of architecture. In Part V it becomes the choice of discriminator, which is why we insist that a discriminator architecture is a reward class in disguise.

8Exit C: what if we do not know $P$?

This is the question that most often derails a first reading, because two entirely different estimation problems are usually collapsed into one. Separate them first.

Two statistical jobs, frequently confused

Job 1 — estimate the expert's statistics $\rho_E$ (or $\mu_E$). Data: the demonstrations. It needs no model and no interaction: it is an average over $\mathcal{D}_E$. Its weakness is support — it says nothing about states the expert never visited.

Job 2 — estimate the current candidate policy's statistics $\rho_{\pi_w}$. This is not in the data at all: $\pi_w$ changes at every gradient step. It requires either a model of $P$ (to compute) or the ability to interact with the environment (to sample). This is the job that makes IRL expensive, and the only one that needs $P$.

Recall from Sections 5.5 and 5.8 that $P$ is consumed in exactly two places, both belonging to Job 2: the expectation over successors in the backward pass (5.19), and the propagation of visitation in the forward pass (5.20). Everything below is about replacing those two sums.

8.2Sampling the gradient

The Monte Carlo replacement is direct. Draw $n$ rollouts $\tau_1,\ldots,\tau_n$ from the current soft policy and set

$$\widehat{\mathbb{E}_{p_w}[\Phi]}=\frac1n\sum_{j=1}^n\Phi(\tau_j),\qquad\widehat{\nabla_wL}=\mu_E-\frac1n\sum_{j=1}^n\Phi(\tau_j),$$(8.1)

which is unbiased and consistent. The catch is that the current soft policy must itself be obtained by an inner forward-RL solve at every outer step — extremely wasteful, since $w$ changes only slightly from one step to the next.

Guided cost learning [Finn et al. 2016] removes the waste with importance sampling: maintain a sampler $\pi_{\mathrm{samp}}$, reuse its rollouts, and correct with self-normalised weights

$$\omega_j\propto\frac{e^{R_\theta(\tau_j)}}{\pi_{\mathrm{samp}}(\tau_j)},\qquad\widehat{\nabla_\theta L}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_E}[\nabla_\theta R_\theta]-\sum_j\frac{\omega_j}{\sum_{j'}\omega_{j'}}\,\nabla_\theta R_\theta(\tau_j),$$(8.2)

interleaving improvement of $\pi_{\mathrm{samp}}$ towards the soft optimum. Here $\pi_{\mathrm{samp}}(\tau_j)$ is shorthand for the product of the sampler's action probabilities along $\tau_j$. Why is that enough — where did the dynamics go?

Step 1: what we want, and what we have

We want $\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim p_\theta}[\nabla_\theta R_\theta(\tau)]$, but we only have samples from the sampler's trajectory distribution $p_{\mathrm{samp}}$. Importance sampling reweights them:

$$\mathbb{E}_{p_\theta}[g]=\mathbb{E}_{p_{\mathrm{samp}}}\Big[\frac{p_\theta(\tau)}{p_{\mathrm{samp}}(\tau)}\,g(\tau)\Big].$$
Step 2: write both trajectory densities out

The model and the sampler share the same dynamics factor $q(\tau)$:

$$p_\theta(\tau)=\frac{q(\tau)\,e^{R_\theta(\tau)}}{Z(\theta)},\qquad p_{\mathrm{samp}}(\tau)=q(\tau)\prod_t\pi_{\mathrm{samp}}(a_t\mid s_t).$$
Step 3: $q$ cancels, so $P$ is never needed
$$\frac{p_\theta(\tau)}{p_{\mathrm{samp}}(\tau)}=\frac{1}{Z(\theta)}\cdot\frac{e^{R_\theta(\tau)}}{\prod_t\pi_{\mathrm{samp}}(a_t\mid s_t)}.$$

Every transition probability has cancelled. The remaining unknown constant $Z(\theta)$ is common to all samples, so it disappears when the weights are normalised by $\sum_{j'}\omega_{j'}$. That is (8.2): computable from rewards and the sampler's own action probabilities alone.

The price: when $\pi_{\mathrm{samp}}$ drifts away from $p_\theta$ the weights degenerate (a few samples carry all the mass), which is the practical failure mode of the method.

8.3Support, exploration, and model error

  • Support. A transition never attempted cannot be estimated. If $\rho_E$ has support the learner never visits (or vice versa), the comparison driving (5.12) is being made on incomparable sets. This is the deep reason imitation degrades off the expert's distribution, and the origin of compounding error in behavioural cloning [Bagnell 2014].
  • Model error compounds. If one fits $\widehat P$ and plans in it, errors accumulate over the effective horizon: a per-step total-variation error $\varepsilon$ produces an occupancy error of order $\varepsilon/(1-\gamma)$ and hence a biased reward gradient. Bias does not average away over gradient steps the way variance does.
  • Interaction may be unacceptable. Exploration in surgery, aviation or finance has costs that no sample-complexity bound captures.
A practical decision rule

Demand exact transition probabilities only when the algorithm asks for exact dynamic-programming expectations. If you can sample transitions, every such expectation can be estimated without ever writing $P$ down. What you pay is sample complexity, variance, a coverage requirement, and possibly interaction risk. What you do not pay is any change to the IRL objective itself: Coordinate 1 is untouched by Exit C.

8.4The pivot: from estimating an expectation to estimating a ratio

Here is the idea that generates the rest of this tutorial.

The gradient (5.12) asks us to compare two expectations of a known function $\phi$ under two unknown distributions, $\rho_E$ and $\rho_\pi$. We have samples from both: the demonstrations give samples of $\rho_E$, and rollouts give samples of $\rho_\pi$. So the real task is: given samples from two distributions, quantify and reduce their discrepancy.

Machine learning has an extremely well-developed tool for exactly that task, and it is not integration. It is binary classification. A classifier trained to distinguish samples of $\rho_E$ from samples of $\rho_\pi$ recovers, at its optimum, the density ratio between them — and the density ratio is precisely the discrepancy signal we wanted. No partition function, no transition kernel, no enumeration.

That substitution is GAIL. We now make it precise.

Part VAdversarial methods

9Occupancy measures: the right change of variables

Sections 4 and 5 both optimise over policies, and both objectives turned out to depend on the policy only through averages of the form $\sum_{s,a}\rho_\pi(s,a)(\cdot)$. That is a strong hint that $\rho$, not $\pi$, is the natural variable. Changing to it converts IRL into a convex-duality problem and makes GAIL a theorem rather than an analogy.

Definition (the occupancy polytope).

$$\mathcal{D}\triangleq\Big\{\rho\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}}_{\ge0}\;:\;\sum_a\rho(s,a)=(1-\gamma)\,d_0(s)+\gamma\sum_{s',a'}P(s\mid s',a')\,\rho(s',a')\quad\forall s\Big\},$$(9.1)

the set of non-negative measures satisfying the Bellman flow constraints: the (discounted) mass flowing out of each state equals the mass starting there plus the mass flowing in.

Theorem (policies and occupancies are the same thing). The map $\pi\mapsto\rho_\pi$ defined by (5.22) is a bijection from stationary policies onto $\mathcal{D}$, with inverse

$$\pi_\rho(a\mid s)=\frac{\rho(s,a)}{\sum_{a'}\rho(s,a')}.$$(9.2)

$\mathcal{D}$ is a compact convex polytope.

This is classical [Puterman 1994; Syed et al. 2008]. (For states that are never reached, the denominator in (9.2) is zero and the policy there is arbitrary; such states do not affect $\rho$.) Three consequences do all the work:

  1. Return is linear in $\rho$: $J_R(\pi)=\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\langle R,\rho_\pi\rangle$ by (5.23). Forward RL is therefore a linear program over $\mathcal{D}$.
  2. Matching occupancy is matching behaviour: by the bijection, $\rho_\pi=\rho_E$ if and only if $\pi=\pi_E$ (on the states the expert visits). Imitation is exactly a distribution-matching problem, with no reward mentioned.
  3. Mixtures are legal: $\mathcal{D}$ is convex, so $\rho_{\tilde\pi}=\sum_i\lambda_i\rho_{\pi_i}$ is realisable — which is what justified the mixed policy returned by Algorithm 1.

Finally, the discounted causal entropy is also a function of $\rho$ alone, and a concave one:

$$\bar{\mathcal{H}}(\rho)\;=\;-\sum_{s,a}\rho(s,a)\log\frac{\rho(s,a)}{\sum_{a'}\rho(s,a')},$$(9.3)

so “entropy-regularised RL” is a concave maximisation over a polytope. This claim is worth deriving, because it is what lets all of Part V move from optimising over policies to optimising over occupancies.

Step 1: write the entropy as a sum over state–action pairs

The discounted causal entropy (6.1) is the discounted expectation of $-\log\pi(a_t\mid s_t)$. Grouping by the pair visited at each time step,

$$\mathcal{H}_\gamma(\pi)=\sum_{s,a}\Big[\sum_{t\ge0}\gamma^t\Pr_\pi(s_t=s,a_t=a)\Big]\big(-\log\pi(a\mid s)\big).$$

The bracket is the occupancy up to normalisation: $\sum_t\gamma^t\Pr_\pi(s_t=s,a_t=a)=\rho_\pi(s,a)/(1-\gamma)$.

Step 2: replace the policy by the occupancy too

By the inverse map (9.2), $\pi(a\mid s)=\rho(s,a)/\sum_{a'}\rho(s,a')$. Substituting,

$$\mathcal{H}_\gamma(\pi)=\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\sum_{s,a}\rho_\pi(s,a)\Big(-\log\frac{\rho_\pi(s,a)}{\sum_{a'}\rho_\pi(s,a')}\Big)=\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\,\bar{\mathcal{H}}(\rho_\pi).$$
Step 3: concavity via the perspective

Fix a state $s$ and write $c_s=\sum_{a'}\rho(s,a')$. That state's contribution to (9.3) is $c_s\cdot H\big(\rho(s,\cdot)/c_s\big)$, where $H$ is the Shannon entropy of a distribution over actions. This is the perspective of the concave function $H$, and perspectives preserve concavity. Summing over states, $\bar{\mathcal{H}}$ is concave in $\rho$.

So every ingredient of the entropy-regularised objective — return (linear) and entropy (concave) — is a function of $\rho$, and the feasible set $\mathcal{D}$ is a polytope. The constant $\frac1{1-\gamma}$ is absorbed into the temperature.

10GAIL: imitation as density-ratio estimation

10.1IRL with an explicit reward regulariser

Following Ho and Ermon's variational treatment (2016), write IRL abstractly. A reward is good if the expert scores well under it and the best achievable policy does not score much better — that is, if the reward explains the gap. Add a convex regulariser $\psi$ on rewards to keep the problem bounded:

$$\mathrm{IRL}_\psi(\pi_E)=\arg\max_R\;-\psi(R)+\Big(\min_\pi\;-\beta\bar{\mathcal{H}}(\rho_\pi)-\langle R,\rho_\pi\rangle\Big)+\langle R,\rho_E\rangle.$$(10.1)

Without $\psi$ this is exactly the ill-posed problem of Section 3: scaling up any reward that separates the expert makes the objective arbitrarily large. $\psi$ is the selection principle, written down explicitly for once.

Theorem (Ho & Ermon 2016). Running RL on the reward returned by (10.1) yields

$$\mathrm{RL}\circ\mathrm{IRL}_\psi(\pi_E)=\arg\min_\pi\;-\beta\bar{\mathcal{H}}(\rho_\pi)+\psi^*(\rho_\pi-\rho_E),$$(10.2)

where $\psi^*$ is the convex conjugate of $\psi$.

Read the theorem slowly

It says that the whole IRL-then-RL pipeline is equivalent to a single occupancy-matching problem, in which the choice of reward regulariser $\psi$ becomes the choice of divergence $\psi^*$ used to compare $\rho_\pi$ with $\rho_E$. The intermediate reward is a computational device that can be eliminated. Everything below is a choice of $\psi$.

10.2Two choices of $\psi$

(i) A constant on a linear class. Let $\psi$ be $0$ if $R=w^\top\phi$ with $\|w\|_2\le1$ and $+\infty$ otherwise. Then $\psi^*$ is the support function of that set, and (10.2) becomes

$$\min_\pi\max_{\|w\|_2\le1}w^\top\big(\mu(\pi)-\mu_E\big)=\min_\pi\|\mu(\pi)-\mu_E\|_2,$$(10.3)

which is apprenticeship learning (Section 4) recovered exactly. Its weakness is now visible as a property of $\psi^*$: it is a hard constraint, forcing exact feature matching within a small class, and it treats any $\rho_\pi$ that differs from $\rho_E$ only outside that class as a perfect match.

(ii) A smooth penalty on the expert's own reward values. Ho and Ermon choose $\psi_{\mathrm{GA}}(R)=\mathbb{E}_{\rho_E}[g(R(s,a))]$ for a particular convex $g$, whose conjugate is a Jensen–Shannon divergence in variational form:

$$\psi_{\mathrm{GA}}^*(\rho_\pi-\rho_E)=\max_{D:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to(0,1)}\;\mathbb{E}_{\rho_E}\big[\log D(s,a)\big]+\mathbb{E}_{\rho_\pi}\big[\log(1-D(s,a))\big].$$(10.4)

This regulariser penalises rewards that are large on expert pairs, is defined for all rewards rather than a fixed span, and — crucially — its conjugate is computable from samples alone.

10.3The GAIL objective

GAIL (Ho & Ermon, 2016)
$$\min_\pi\max_D\;\;\mathbb{E}_{(s,a)\sim\rho_E}\big[\log D(s,a)\big]+\mathbb{E}_{(s,a)\sim\rho_\pi}\big[\log\big(1-D(s,a)\big)\big]-\beta\bar{\mathcal{H}}(\rho_\pi).$$(10.5)

Convention, fixed for the rest of this tutorial: $D(s,a)$ is the estimated probability that the pair came from the expert. (Ho and Ermon's paper uses the opposite labelling; the two objectives are related by $D\mapsto1-D$. Mixing the conventions is the single most common source of sign confusion in this literature.)

Proposition (the discriminator estimates a density ratio). For fixed $\pi$, the inner maximum of (10.5) is attained at

$$D^\star(s,a)=\frac{\rho_E(s,a)}{\rho_E(s,a)+\rho_\pi(s,a)},$$(10.6)

and the optimal value equals $2\,\mathrm{JS}(\rho_E\|\rho_\pi)-\log4$. Hence GAIL is

$$\min_\pi\;2\,\mathrm{JS}(\rho_E\|\rho_\pi)-\beta\bar{\mathcal{H}}(\rho_\pi).$$(10.7)
Step 1: for fixed $\pi$, the inner problem is pointwise

Write both expectations as sums over pairs. Since $D$ can be chosen independently at every $(s,a)$, we maximise separately, for each pair,

$$\rho_E(s,a)\log D+\rho_\pi(s,a)\log(1-D),\qquad D\in(0,1).$$

A non-negative combination of $\log D$ and $\log(1-D)$ is concave in $D$, so a stationary point is the maximum.

Step 2: set the derivative to zero
$$\frac{\rho_E}{D}-\frac{\rho_\pi}{1-D}=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\rho_E(1-D)=\rho_\pi D.$$
Step 3: solve for the optimal discriminator
$$D^\star(s,a)=\frac{\rho_E(s,a)}{\rho_E(s,a)+\rho_\pi(s,a)}.$$

This is the posterior probability of “expert” under equal priors, and its log-odds are the log density ratio: $\log\frac{D^\star}{1-D^\star}=\log\frac{\rho_E}{\rho_\pi}$.

Step 4: substitute back to obtain Jensen–Shannon

With the mixture $m=\frac12(\rho_E+\rho_\pi)$, we have $\frac{\rho_E}{\rho_E+\rho_\pi}=\frac{\rho_E}{2m}$, so the optimal value is

$$\sum_{s,a}\Big[\rho_E\log\frac{\rho_E}{2m}+\rho_\pi\log\frac{\rho_\pi}{2m}\Big]=\mathrm{KL}(\rho_E\|m)+\mathrm{KL}(\rho_\pi\|m)-2\log2=2\,\mathrm{JS}(\rho_E\|\rho_\pi)-\log4.$$

The policy therefore minimises a Jensen–Shannon divergence between occupancies, minus the entropy bonus. An intractable integral has been replaced by a classification problem. ∎

10.4Turning the discriminator into a reward

The policy player minimises $\mathbb{E}_{\rho_\pi}[\log(1-D)]$; equivalently, it maximises the return of the surrogate reward

$$\hat r(s,a)=-\log\big(1-D(s,a)\big)\;\in\;(0,\infty).$$(10.8)

Any policy-gradient method (TRPO, PPO) can then be run on $\hat r$.

Surrogate rewards are not interchangeable

Three surrogates appear in practice, and they are different functions:

SurrogateRangeImplicit bias
$-\log(1-D)$$(0,+\infty)$always positive: encourages surviving longer
$\log D$$(-\infty,0)$always negative: encourages terminating sooner
$\log D-\log(1-D)$$(-\infty,\infty)$the true log-odds; unbiased in sign

On tasks with variable episode length these choices change the learned behaviour markedly, independently of anything to do with imitation. A text that says “use the log-odds” and an equation that uses $-\log(1-D)$ are not describing the same algorithm. AIRL uses the log-odds form, for reasons that will turn out to be structural rather than cosmetic.

Algorithm 3. GAIL (Ho & Ermon, 2016)
  1. Require: expert data $\mathcal{D}_E$, an environment sampler (no $P$ needed), initial policy $\pi_\vartheta$, discriminator $D_\varphi$
  2. for $k=1,2,\ldots$ do
  3. collect rollouts $\mathcal{B}\sim\pi_\vartheta$ by interacting with the environment
  4. discriminator step: ascend $\nabla_\varphi\big[\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_E}\log D_\varphi+\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{B}}\log(1-D_\varphi)\big]$
  5. policy step: one TRPO/PPO update on the reward $\hat r=-\log(1-D_\varphi)$ with entropy bonus $\beta$
  6. end for
  7. return $\pi_\vartheta$

10.5What GAIL does not give you

At convergence, the discriminator contains no reward information

If imitation succeeds, then $\rho_\pi=\rho_E$, so $D^\star\equiv\frac12$ and the surrogate reward (10.8) is the constant $\log2$. A constant reward makes every policy optimal (Section 3). There is therefore literally nothing to extract at the end of a successful GAIL run.

Any reward-like signal GAIL exhibits exists only during training, as a by-product of the current mismatch between learner and expert. It is a function of the optimisation trajectory, not of the task. This — not a vague appeal to “shaping terms” — is the precise reason GAIL does not transfer.

11AIRL: recovering something that transfers

AIRL [Fu, Luo & Levine 2018] keeps GAIL's sampling machinery and repairs its Coordinate-1 deficit by constraining the discriminator, so that a transferable reward is forced to appear inside it.

11.1Where the discriminator's form comes from

The AIRL discriminator is not an arbitrary architecture. Suppose the “expert” distribution is the maximum-entropy model $p_\theta(\tau)\propto q(\tau)e^{\sum_tf_\theta(s_t,a_t)}$ of Section 5, and the “fake” samples come from the current policy $\pi$. The Bayes-optimal discriminator between the two can be derived in three steps.

Step 1: the Bayes-optimal discriminator is a posterior

With equal priors on the two sources, the best possible classifier outputs the posterior probability that a sample came from the expert model:

$$D(\tau)=\frac{p_\theta(\tau)}{p_\theta(\tau)+p_\pi(\tau)}.$$
Step 2: factor both trajectory densities
$$p_\theta(\tau)\propto q(\tau)\,e^{\sum_tf_\theta(s_t,a_t)},\qquad p_\pi(\tau)=q(\tau)\prod_t\pi(a_t\mid s_t).$$

The key observation: both contain the same dynamics factor $q(\tau)=d_0(s_0)\prod_tP(s_{t+1}\mid s_t,a_t)$. The two hypotheses agree completely about how the environment moves; they disagree only about how actions are chosen.

Step 3: divide, and the dynamics cancel
$$\frac{p_\theta(\tau)}{p_\pi(\tau)}\propto\prod_t\frac{e^{f_\theta(s_t,a_t)}}{\pi(a_t\mid s_t)},\qquad\log\frac{p_\theta(\tau)}{p_\pi(\tau)}=\text{const}+\sum_t\Big[f_\theta(s_t,a_t)-\log\pi(a_t\mid s_t)\Big].$$

The trajectory-level log-odds decompose into a sum of per-step terms, each of the form $f_\theta-\log\pi$. Taking the per-transition version of the posterior gives the AIRL discriminator:

$$D_\theta(s,a)=\frac{\exp f_\theta(s,a)}{\exp f_\theta(s,a)+\pi(a\mid s)}.$$(11.1)

Two facts follow at once.

Proposition (the AIRL reward is the logit minus a log-policy). With $D$ as in (11.1), the log-odds reward is

$$\hat r(s,a)=\log D_\theta(s,a)-\log\big(1-D_\theta(s,a)\big)=f_\theta(s,a)-\log\pi(a\mid s).$$(11.2)
Remark: the $-\log\pi$ term is not noise

Maximising $\mathbb{E}_\pi\big[\sum_t\gamma^t(f-\log\pi)\big]$ is exactly maximising $J_f(\pi)+\mathcal{H}_\gamma(\pi)$: the entropy bonus of (6.1) appears automatically. The adversarial game is therefore performing entropy-regularised RL on $f$ whether or not anyone adds an entropy term by hand. At the optimum, $f_\theta$ recovers the advantage $A_{\mathrm{soft}}(s,a)=Q_{\mathrm{soft}}(s,a)-V_{\mathrm{soft}}(s)$ of the expert's soft policy — and this is the crux of what follows, because an advantage is a reward plus shaping, not a reward.

11.2The structural constraint

AIRL restricts $f$ to a shaped form:

$$f_{\theta,\omega}(s,a,s')=\underbrace{g_\theta(s)}_{\text{task reward}}\;+\;\underbrace{\gamma\,h_\omega(s')-h_\omega(s)}_{\text{potential-based shaping}}.$$(11.3)
$g_\theta$ must depend on the state only

This is the load-bearing assumption of AIRL, and it is very often mis-stated as $g_\theta(s,a)$. Here is why it must be $g_\theta(s)$.

For fixed dynamics $P$, the rewards inducing the same soft-optimal policy are exactly those of the form

$$r'(s,a)=r(s,a)+\gamma\,\mathbb{E}_{s'\sim P(\cdot\mid s,a)}\big[\Psi(s')\big]-\Psi(s)$$(11.4)

for some potential $\Psi$. Note that the shaping term $\gamma\,\mathbb{E}_{s'\mid s,a}[\Psi(s')]-\Psi(s)$ depends on $a$ and depends on $P$. So a state–action reward is identified only up to a nuisance term that (i) cannot be distinguished from the reward by any amount of behavioural data, and (ii) changes when the dynamics change. That is precisely a failure of transfer.

If instead $g$ is a function of $s$ alone, the only member of the family (11.4) that is itself state-only is the one with $\Psi\equiv$ const (under the condition below). The action-dependent freedom is squeezed entirely into $h_\omega$, and $g_\theta$ is pinned down up to an additive constant.

Definition (decomposability). Call two states one-step linked if some state has positive-probability transitions to both. The dynamics are decomposable if the transitive closure of this relation connects all of $\mathcal{S}$. Equivalently: any two functions $a(s),b(s)$ with $a(s)+b(s')$ constant along all feasible transitions must themselves be constant.

Theorem (disentanglement; Fu et al. 2018). Suppose the dynamics are decomposable, the discriminator is trained to optimality with $f$ constrained as in (11.3), and the true expert is soft-optimal for a state-only reward $r^\star(s)$. Then at the global optimum there is a constant $c$ with

$$g_\theta(s)=r^\star(s)+c,\qquad h_\omega(s)=V^\star_{\mathrm{soft}}(s)+c.$$(11.5)

In particular, $g_\theta$ is invariant to changes in the transition dynamics, so re-optimising $g_\theta$ in a different MDP with the same reward structure recovers appropriate behaviour there.

Remark: what the theorem does and does not say

It says: under a structural condition on the dynamics, a state-only reward assumption, and exact optimisation, the recovered $g$ equals the ground-truth reward up to a constant. It does not say that the reward is uniquely recoverable in general. The state-only restriction is doing the identification, and it is an assumption about the world, not a fact derived from data. If the true reward genuinely depends on actions (an energy cost, say), AIRL will misattribute that dependence to $h_\omega$ and the recovered $g$ will be wrong. Section 3 has not been repealed; it has been paid off with an extra assumption — and the whole point of this tutorial is that this is always the trade.

Algorithm 4. AIRL (Fu, Luo & Levine, 2018)
  1. Require: expert data $\mathcal{D}_E$, an environment sampler, policy $\pi_\vartheta$, networks $g_\theta$ and $h_\omega$
  2. for $k=1,2,\ldots$ do
  3. collect rollouts $\mathcal{B}\sim\pi_\vartheta$
  4. form $f_{\theta,\omega}(s,a,s')=g_\theta(s)+\gamma h_\omega(s')-h_\omega(s)$ and $D=\dfrac{e^f}{e^f+\pi_\vartheta(a\mid s)}$
  5. discriminator step: ascend the binary log-likelihood of $D$ on $\mathcal{D}_E$ (label 1) versus $\mathcal{B}$ (label 0), with respect to $(\theta,\omega)$
  6. policy step: update $\pi_\vartheta$ by TRPO/PPO on $\hat r=\log D-\log(1-D)=f_{\theta,\omega}-\log\pi_\vartheta$
  7. end for
  8. return the transferable reward $g_\theta$, the policy $\pi_\vartheta$, and the soft value estimate $h_\omega$
Ledger · AIRL
Assumed (Coordinate 1)
Everything GAIL assumes, plus: the true reward is state-only, and the dynamics are decomposable. In exchange, the shaping ambiguity — the one ambiguity that blocks transfer — is quotiented out by construction.
Approximated (Coordinate 2)
As for GAIL: samples throughout, no $Z$, no $P$. Additionally requires evaluating $\pi(a\mid s)$ explicitly inside $D$, so the policy must have a tractable density.
Delivers
$g_\theta\approx r^\star+c$, re-optimisable under new dynamics; and $h_\omega\approx V^\star_{\mathrm{soft}}$, a useful by-product in its own right.
Fails when
The reward really depends on actions; the dynamics are not decomposable; or the discriminator is not trained near optimality — the theorem is about the global optimum of a minimax problem, which is not what any optimiser reliably finds.

The one-paragraph version

Max-margin fixed the ambiguity geometrically and paid with a brittle behaviour model. Maximum entropy replaced the geometry with a likelihood, which handled noise and gave a convex problem, and paid with an intractable partition function. Occupancy measures revealed that both were solving a distribution-matching problem in disguise. GAIL then estimated that distributional discrepancy by classification instead of integration, which made it scale — at the cost of throwing the reward away entirely. AIRL put a reward back by constraining the classifier so that a state-only component and a shaping component are forced apart, buying transfer with one more assumption. At no point did anyone recover a true reward from data alone, and at no point did anyone claim to.

13The three questions, answered on one page

Almost every beginner arrives with the same three questions. Having built the machinery, we can now answer them crisply.

Q1. “Everything is finite here — what happens in the real, infinite case?”

The statistics are unchanged; only the computation changes. An infinite horizon needs discounted causal entropy and a soft Bellman fixed point, which still contracts (the log-sum-exp lemma of Section 6.1), so nothing is lost. A large or continuous $\mathcal{S}$ destroys the tabular passes and forces function approximation and sampling. A continuous $\mathcal{A}$ turns the soft maximum over actions into an intractable integral, handled variationally. And on a continuum, the maximum-entropy objective is only well posed as a relative entropy $-\mathrm{KL}(p\|q)$ — which is why we insisted on the reference measure $q$ from the very first line, even though in the finite deterministic case it is a harmless constant. The reason to learn IRL discretely first is not that discrete problems matter; it is that in the discrete case every quantity is definable, so you can see precisely which ones later become approximations. (Section 6)

Q2. “Do the features have to be hand-designed?”

No. The maximum-entropy learning rule is $\mathbb{E}_{\text{expert}}[\nabla_\theta R_\theta]-\mathbb{E}_{\text{model}}[\nabla_\theta R_\theta]$ for any differentiable reward class; the classical rule $\mu_E-\mathbb{E}[\Phi]$ is the special case $R_\theta=w^\top\phi$. What a fixed linear $\phi$ actually bought was a finite sufficient statistic, a convex dual, a class-wide performance guarantee and interpretability; what it cost was silent misspecification. Learning $\phi$ trades convexity for expressiveness and introduces a new $(w,\phi)\sim(A^{-\top}w,A\phi)$ ambiguity. Most importantly, the choice never disappears: in GAIL and AIRL the discriminator architecture is the reward class, chosen less visibly. (Section 7)

Q3. “What if we don't know $P$ and have to explore?”

First separate the two jobs. Estimating the expert's statistics needs no model at all — it is an average over the demonstrations. Estimating the current candidate policy's statistics is the only place $P$ was ever used in exact MaxEnt IRL: the successor expectation in the soft backward pass (5.19) and the visitation propagation in the forward pass (5.20). Both are expectations, so both can be replaced by sampling whenever you can draw transitions, even if you can never write $P$ down. You then pay in variance, sample complexity, coverage and possibly the real-world risk of exploring; you do not pay any change in the IRL objective itself. Pushed one step further, the observation that we only ever needed to compare two sampled distributions replaces integration by classification — and that step is GAIL. (Section 8)

14A checklist for reading an IRL paper

  1. What is the behaviour model? Exact optimality, Boltzmann rationality (with which temperature?), or something else?
  2. What is the reward class, including when it is hidden in an architecture?
  3. What is the selection principle, and is it stated or smuggled in?
  4. Which quantity plays the role of $\mathbb{E}_{\text{model}}[\cdot]$, and how is it obtained — exact DP, rollouts, importance weights, or a discriminator?
  5. Does the method need $P$, a simulator, online interaction, or only offline data?
  6. What exactly is claimed to be recovered — a policy, a reward equivalence class, or a specific reward — and under what conditions?
  7. If transfer is claimed, which ambiguity was removed to make it possible, and by what assumption?

15Common errors, collected

These mistakes recur in secondary expositions, including earlier drafts of these notes. Each is discussed at the section linked.

  1. Deriving $p(\tau)\propto e^{w^\top\Phi(\tau)}$ and later asserting $p(\tau)\propto q(\tau)e^{w^\top\Phi(\tau)}$. These solve different programs. Put the reference measure into the objective from the start (Section 5.1).
  2. Writing the maximum-entropy program as $-\int p\log p$ on a continuous trajectory space. Differential entropy is not reparameterisation-invariant (Section 6.2).
  3. Using the naive soft backward pass under stochastic dynamics. It is risk-seeking and implicitly rewrites the transition kernel (5.16); use maximum causal entropy (Section 5.7).
  4. Attributing potential-based shaping to Ng & Russell (2000). It is Ng, Harada & Russell (1999), and the converse — that this is the only dynamics-independent invariance — is what makes it the right nuisance class (Section 3.1).
  5. Returning the last policy from Abbeel and Ng's algorithm. The guarantee is for a mixture (Section 4.3).
  6. Mixing GAIL discriminator conventions, or treating $-\log(1-D)$, $\log D$ and the log-odds as interchangeable. They induce different survival biases (Section 10.4).
  7. Claiming that GAIL learns a reward. At convergence $D\equiv\frac12$ (Section 10.5).
  8. Writing AIRL's task term as $g_\theta(s,a)$. The disentanglement theorem requires a state-only $g$ (Section 11.2).
  9. Confusing uniqueness of $V^\star$ with identifiability of $R$, or uniqueness of the estimator $w$ with identifiability of the estimand (Section 3 and Section 5.4).

16Conclusion

Inverse reinforcement learning is easy to state and impossible to solve as stated. The forward map from reward to behaviour destroys information, so behaviour can never determine reward; and the natural repairs all require an expectation under the policy that a candidate reward induces, which is a full forward-RL problem nested inside every gradient step.

Reading the field through those two failures makes its history legible. Max-margin and maximum entropy are two answers to the first failure, and maximum entropy wins because it produces a likelihood: noise is modelled rather than excluded, and the estimation problem becomes convex. The exact tabular algorithm of Section 5 is the reference implementation of that idea, and its two passes show, with no ambiguity, where a transition model is required. Every method after it is an answer to the second failure. Occupancy measures reveal what the inner expectation really is; sampling replaces the forward pass; importance weighting reuses samples; and finally the realisation that we only ever needed to compare two sampled distributions replaces integration with classification, which is GAIL. AIRL then returns to the first failure with the leverage that adversarial training provides, and buys back exactly one ambiguity — potential shaping — at the price of exactly one assumption — a state-only reward.

The lesson worth carrying away is not any single algorithm. It is that in IRL, every capability is purchased with an assumption, and the assumptions are always visible if you look for them in the right two places.
