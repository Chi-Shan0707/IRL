Inverse Reinforcement Learning

What Must Be Assumed, and What Must Be Approximated

Teaching edition — the second half slows down and derives everything step by step.

Frame the lecture with one sentence: IRL is easy to state and impossible to solve as stated. These slides follow the handout by Yuhan Chi (Fudan University). Everything is organised on two coordinates: what a method ASSUMES, and what it APPROXIMATES. The first half is a brisk review; from the maximum-entropy algorithm onward we slow down and derive each step.
ACT I · SETUP

The Well-Posed and the Ill-Posed

Why forward RL is easy, and the two independent ways its inverse fails.

Act I is the setup. We recall why the forward problem is well posed, then see precisely what the inverse problem destroys. Keep this part brisk — the pacing slows after the maximum-entropy algorithm.
Act I · The forward direction

Forward RL is well-posed

Given a reward, optimality is a fixed point of a contraction, and that contraction computes it.

$(\mathcal{B}^\star V)(s)=\max_a\big[R(s,a)+\gamma\textstyle\sum_{s'}P(s'\mid s,a)V(s')\big]$
$\|\mathcal{B}^\star V_1-\mathcal{B}^\star V_2\|_\infty \le \gamma\,\|V_1-V_2\|_\infty$

⇒ unique $V^\star$, geometric convergence, greedy $=$ optimal. Evaluation is even easier — it is a linear solve:

$\mathbf{V}^\pi=(\mathbf{I}-\gamma\mathbf{P}^\pi)^{-1}\mathbf{R}^\pi$
Two facts to carry forward: (1) optimality is a contraction, so the answer is unique and computable; (2) evaluation is just a linear solve, and the Neumann series shows value is an occupancy-weighted sum of rewards. That second reading is the bridge to GAIL later.
Act I · The forward direction

Four pillars hold forward RL up

1 · Finite-dimensional unknown, exact equations

$V^\star\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}|}$ solves a finite linear system.

2 · The solution operator is a contraction

Uniqueness and a geometric convergence rate come for free.

3 · Expectations are finite sums over a known kernel

Every $\sum_{s'}P(s'\mid s,a)(\cdot)$ can be evaluated exactly.

4 · The objective is given

There is a ground truth against which any answer can be scored.

IRL removes pillar 4 (Coordinate 1). Large-scale or unknown environments remove pillars 1–3 (Coordinate 2).

This is the organising image of the whole lecture. Point at each pillar as it appears. Everything later in the deck is about which pillar a method loses and what it buys the loss with.
Act I · The inverse direction

The inverse question

Forward RL: dynamics $\mathcal{M}$ + reward $R$  $\xrightarrow{\text{optimise}}$  optimal policy $\pi^\star$

Inverse RL: dynamics $\mathcal{M}$ + demonstrations $\mathcal{D}_E$  $\xrightarrow{\text{infer}}$  reward $R$ (then usually $\pi$)

Assumption underneath it all: $\mathcal{D}_E$ was generated by a policy $\pi_E$ that is optimal, or near-optimal, for some unknown $R^\star$.

Taken literally, that assumption yields only inequalities — $J_R(\pi_E)\ge J_R(\pi)$ for all $\pi$. A system of inequalities is a weak thing to estimate from. Much of modern IRL consists of replacing them by a probabilistic behaviour model, which turns inequalities into a likelihood that a gradient can act on.

Motivate with domains where writing a reward is harder than demonstrating: driving, surgery, aerobatic flight, dialogue. Hand-specified rewards get mis-specified, and optimising a mis-specified reward gives reward hacking. Then stress the key move: inequalities to likelihood. That move is exactly what maximum entropy does, in Act II.
Act I · The spine of these notes

IRL fails in two independent ways at once

Coordinate 1 · statistically

Infinitely many rewards explain the same behaviour. No amount of data can single one out without extra assumptions.

Coordinate 2 · computationally

Every principled way of scoring a candidate reward needs an expectation under the policy that reward induces — a full forward RL problem nested inside every gradient step.

The two are genuinely independent. A method can be statistically honest and computationally hopeless (exact tabular MaxEnt in a large MDP), or computationally excellent and statistically silent about reward (GAIL).

When someone says “IRL is ill-posed”, ask which coordinate they mean.

This is the single most important slide of Act I. The two coordinates are the spine of the handout; every method gets located on them, and each method section closes with a Ledger box recording both.
Act I · What can be inverted?

Three objects that must not be confused

1

$V^\star$ is unique, given $\mathcal{M}$ and $R$: it is the fixed point of a contraction.

2

$\pi^\star$ need not be unique: ties in the greedy step give a set $\Pi^\star(R)$.

3

$R$ is far from unique given behaviour: $\Pi^\star$ has enormous fibres.

Uniqueness of the forward solution says nothing whatever about uniqueness of the inverse. These are three statements about three different maps.

The classic trap: “inverse optimisation” presumes injectivity. The forward map R -> Pi*(R) is drastically many-to-one. Before choosing an algorithm we should know precisely what information the map destroys.
Act I · What can be inverted?

The solution set is a convex cone

Ng & Russell (2000): a deterministic policy $\pi$ (say $\pi(s)\equiv a_1$) is optimal for a state reward $R$ iff, for every other action $a$,

$(\mathbf{P}_{a_1}-\mathbf{P}_a)\,(\mathbf{I}-\gamma\mathbf{P}_{a_1})^{-1}R \;\succeq\; 0$

So the consistent rewards form a closed convex cone: closed under addition and non-negative scaling.

It always contains $R\equiv 0$, for which every policy is optimal. Observing behaviour alone can never exclude the degenerate explanation “nothing matters”.

The cone picture is worth drawing in the air with your hands: a wedge of consistent rewards, closed under scaling, always touching the origin. This is the precise statement of Coordinate 1.
Act I · A nuisance parameter

Potential shaping preserves optimality

For any potential $\Psi:\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}$, the shaped reward

$R'(s,a,s') = R(s,a) + \gamma\Psi(s') - \Psi(s)$

shifts every policy’s value by the same constant, so it preserves the entire preference ordering:

$\Pi^\star(R') = \Pi^\star(R)$
Attribution

This is Ng, Harada & Russell (1999), not the 2000 IRL paper. That work also proves the converse: potential shaping is the ONLY additive reward modification that preserves optimality for all transition kernels. That converse is exactly why it is the right “nuisance parameter” in IRL — and it is the ambiguity AIRL will quotient away.

Foreshadow AIRL here without giving it away: “remember this ambiguity; in Act IV a method will buy back exactly this one with exactly one assumption.”
Act I · Partial identification

A minimal example

One state, one decision between left and right. The expert chooses left. All we learn is

$R(s,\text{left}) \;\ge\; R(s,\text{right})$

The pairs $(1,0)$, $(10,-3)$ and $(0,-100)$ are equally consistent.

One observation cannot pin down two numbers — and repeating the same observation never helps: more demonstrations add inequalities, which sharpen the preference structure but never fix an absolute scale.

But note

It DOES rule out $R(\text{left}) < R(\text{right})$. IRL is not hopeless — it is partially identifying.

This example is the honest anchor for the whole identification discussion. Emphasise the last point: IRL is partially identifying, not useless. The honest outputs are on the next slide.
Act I · What IRL may honestly deliver

The honest target: three outputs

1

An equivalence class of rewards inducing the observed behaviour.

2

A single representative chosen by an explicit selection principle (max-margin, max-entropy, a Bayesian prior, sparsity).

3

Or simply a policy whose occupancy measure matches the expert’s, with no claim about reward at all.

Item 3 is imitation learning, and is often what one actually wants. Item 2 is what most papers deliver. Item 1 is what the data actually supports.

Be suspicious of

“Our method recovers the true reward function.” No method does that from behaviour alone. The useful question is never “is the reward right?” but “is it right for the use I have in mind?” — reproducing behaviour in the same MDP and transferring behaviour to a new MDP need very different amounts of identification.

Confusing 2 with 1 is the single most common overclaim in the literature. Cao et al. 2021 and Skalse et al. 2023 make the partial-identifiability picture precise: even with infinite data and a known model, the reward is determined only up to characterisable transformations.
Act I · Making the problem finite

Choosing a reward class

$R:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}$ is a function — infinite-dimensional if the spaces are continuous. A finite vector $w\in\mathbb{R}^d$ appears only after we choose a class, most commonly the linear one:

$R_w(s,a) = w^\top\phi(s,a)$

Then return collapses into a finite statistic:

$J_{R_w}(\pi) = w^\top\,\mathbb{E}_\pi\Big[\textstyle\sum_{t\ge0}\gamma^t\phi(s_t,a_t)\Big] = w^\top\mu(\pi)$

The vector $\mu(\pi)\in\mathbb{R}^d$ is a sufficient statistic of the policy for every reward in the class. Everything else about $\pi$ is irrelevant to $w$.

This algebraic fact is what makes classical IRL tractable — and losing it is the price of leaving the linear class (Exit B).

This is a modelling commitment on Coordinate 1, not a law of IRL. Alternatives: kernel/GP rewards, neural rewards, and — importantly — reward classes defined implicitly by a discriminator architecture. That last one becomes central in Act IV.
ACT II · TWO SELECTION PRINCIPLES, COMPUTED EXACTLY

The Safe Corner

Finite spaces, a known transition kernel, fixed features. Here Coordinate 2 costs nothing — so we can think only about Coordinate 1.

Define the safe corner precisely: finite S and A, P fully known, phi fixed in advance. Every expectation in this part is a finite sum we can actually evaluate. This is the only part where every number can be checked by hand — and the objects defined here reappear, in approximated form, in every method that follows.
Act II · Selection principle I

Max-Margin: the idea

Abbeel & Ng (2004): among all rewards consistent with the data, prefer the one under which the expert beats the alternatives by the largest margin.

$\max_{\xi,w}\ \xi \quad\text{s.t.}\quad w^\top\mu_E \;\ge\; w^\top\mu(\pi_i)+\xi,\quad i=0,\dots,k-1,\qquad \|w\|_2\le1$

The norm constraint is necessary: scaling $w\mapsto\alpha w$ scales any margin, so without it the problem is unbounded.

This is the hard-margin SVM in disguise — $\mu_E$ is the single positive example, $\{\mu(\pi_i)\}$ the negatives. Its dual is the distance problem

$\min_{\bar\mu\in\mathrm{conv}\{\mu(\pi_i)\}} \|\mu_E-\bar\mu\|_2, \qquad w\propto\mu_E-\bar\mu$

Geometric content: the reward direction always points from the best current imitation towards the expert.

Two-player reading: a reward player solves the QP, a policy player solves a full forward RL problem for the resulting reward, and they alternate. Solving the dual directly is Abbeel and Ng's "projection" variant, which avoids a QP solver.
Act II · Why feature matching is enough

Feature matching matches return

Lemma

If $\|\mu(\pi)-\mu_E\|_2\le\varepsilon$, then for every $w$ with $\|w\|_2\le1$:

$\big|J_{R_w}(\pi)-w^\top\mu_E\big| = \big|w^\top(\mu(\pi)-\mu_E)\big| \le \varepsilon$  (Cauchy–Schwarz)

Matching feature expectations matches expected return simultaneously for every reward in the linear class — without knowing which one is the truth.

This lemma is why apprenticeship learning can sidestep identification. It also states its own limitation with unusual clarity: the guarantee is quantified over $w\in\mathbb{R}^d$ and says nothing about rewards outside $\mathrm{span}\{\phi_1,\dots,\phi_d\}$.

This is the elegant heart of apprenticeship learning: a d-dimensional summary certifies performance for a whole class at once. But the guarantee is only as good as the class — remember this when we reach Exit B.
Act II · A frequently dropped theorem

The output is a mixture, not the last policy

Common error

The algorithm does not return the last $\pi_k$. What is provably close to $\mu_E$ is a point in the convex hull of the $\mu(\pi_i)$, and convex combinations of feature expectations are realised by randomising over policies, not by any single one of them.

This works because occupancy measures mix linearly:

$\rho_{\tilde\pi} = \sum_i \lambda_i\,\rho_{\pi_i}$

Presentations that return “the final policy” have quietly dropped the theorem.

The returned object is a mixed policy: sample i ~ lambda* once at t=0, then follow pi_i forever. The guarantee (O(d/((1-gamma)^2 eps^2)) iterations, notably independent of |S|) is for the mixture.
Act II · Ledger

Max-Margin IRL — the ledger

Assumed (Coordinate 1). The expert is exactly optimal; the reward is linear in a fixed $\phi$; ties are broken by maximal margin.

Approximated (Coordinate 2). Nothing, in the safe corner: $\mu(\pi)$ comes from an exact linear solve and the inner RL is exact. Cost: one full forward-RL solve and one QP per iteration.

Delivers. A mixed policy matching $\mu_E$, plus a reward direction $w$ whose interpretation is weak.

Limitations, stated precisely: ties are broken arbitrarily; a sub-optimal expert breaks it (the feasible set shrinks or empties, and there is no likelihood to absorb noise); the inner solve is a full forward RL problem per iteration; and it is silent about which reward is right.

The last limitation is the setup for maximum entropy: a noisy expert makes strict inequalities infeasible, and there is no way to model that noise. MaxEnt replaces inequalities with a likelihood precisely to fix this.
Act II · Selection principle II

Maximum Entropy: the motivation

Max-margin treats the expert as infallible. Real demonstrations are noisy — and worse, systematically noisy: an expert indifferent between two routes takes each about half the time.

Ziebart et al. (2008) replace the inequality model by a probabilistic one, then select among the surviving models by maximum entropy.

Setting for this whole section

Finite $\mathcal{S}$ and $\mathcal{A}$, finite horizon $T$, known kernel $P$, and $\gamma=1$ with $\Phi(\tau)=\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\phi(s_t,a_t)$. Discounting and infinite horizons come back in Act III.

The behaviour-model upgrade is the conceptual move: from "the expert is best" to "the expert is exponentially more likely to choose better". That single change is what buys noise handling, a likelihood, and convexity.
Act II · Where most treatments go wrong

Entropy of what, exactly?

We want a model $p(\tau)$ that (i) reproduces the expert’s feature statistics and (ii) commits to nothing else. Requirement (ii) is what “maximum entropy” formalises. But entropy relative to what?

A trajectory is produced by two agencies: the learner, who chooses actions, and the environment, who chooses successor states. Maximising plain Shannon entropy declares every trajectory equally likely a priori — including trajectories the environment would essentially never produce. That is obviously wrong.

Definition · dynamics-induced reference measure

$q(\tau) \triangleq d_0(s_0)\prod_{t=0}^{T-1}P(s_{t+1}\mid s_t,a_t)$

$q$ is a measure, not a distribution: $\sum_\tau q(\tau)=|\mathcal{A}|^T$. It integrates out the environment but not the actions. Nothing below requires $q$ to be normalised.

Go slowly here — this is one of the subtle points the handout insists on. q is "uniform over your own choices, honest about nature's". When P is deterministic and d_0 uniform, q is constant and relative entropy reduces to Shannon entropy up to a constant. That special case is what most expositions present; it is not the general case.
Act II · Why the reference measure matters

Two errors this definition prevents

1 · The retrofit error

Maximising plain Shannon entropy gives $p(\tau)\propto e^{w^\top\Phi(\tau)}$ with no dynamics factor. Many expositions derive that, then later assert without comment that “really” $p(\tau)\propto q(\tau)e^{w^\top\Phi(\tau)}$. These are solutions to two different programs. Putting $q$ in from line one makes derivation and formula agree.

2 · The continuum error

Starting from $-\int p\log p\,d\tau$ over a continuous trajectory space gives a differential entropy: not reparameterisation-invariant, not bounded above. The relative entropy $-\mathrm{KL}(p\|q)$ has neither defect — which is why we insist on it now, even though in the finite deterministic case $q$ is a harmless constant.

These are errors 1 and 2 on the "common errors" list near the end. If the audience remembers one thing from this slide, let it be: the reference measure is not a correction applied later, it is in the objective from the first line.
Act II · The program

The primal program

$\max_p\ \ \mathcal{H}_q(p) = -\textstyle\sum_\tau p(\tau)\log\frac{p(\tau)}{q(\tau)}$
$\text{s.t.}\quad \sum_\tau p(\tau)\Phi(\tau)=\mu_E$  (feature matching)
$\phantom{\text{s.t.}}\quad\ \ \sum_\tau p(\tau)=1,\quad p(\tau)\ge0$  (normalisation)

The feasible set is the simplex intersected with $d$ affine constraints, and $\mathcal{H}_q$ is strictly concave on it, so the solution — if the feasible set is non-empty — is unique.

Note carefully: this uniqueness is over distributions, not over rewards. The identifiability problem of Act I still applies.

Set up the derivation on the next slides. The two constraint types give two Lagrange multipliers: a vector w for features, a scalar c for normalisation.
Act II · Variational derivation · steps 1–2

Solving the primal: Lagrangian & stationarity

Step 1 · Write the Lagrangian

Multiplier $w\in\mathbb{R}^d$ for features, $c\in\mathbb{R}$ for normalisation:

$\mathcal{L}(p,w,c) = -\sum_\tau p(\tau)\log\frac{p(\tau)}{q(\tau)} + w^\top\Big(\sum_\tau p(\tau)\Phi(\tau)-\mu_E\Big) + c\Big(\sum_\tau p(\tau)-1\Big)$

Step 2 · Stationarity in $p(\tau)$

For a fixed $\tau$, differentiate with respect to the scalar $p(\tau)$, using $\frac{\partial}{\partial p}\big[-p\log\tfrac{p}{q}\big]=-\log\tfrac{p}{q}-1$:

$\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial p(\tau)} = -\log\frac{p(\tau)}{q(\tau)} - 1 + w^\top\Phi(\tau) + c = 0$

Do not rush the derivative. The term -p log(p/q) differentiates to -log(p/q) - 1 by the product rule; the rest is linear in p. Each tau gives one independent scalar equation.
Act II · Variational derivation · steps 3–4

Solving and normalising

Step 3 · Solve for $p(\tau)$

$\log\frac{p(\tau)}{q(\tau)} = w^\top\Phi(\tau)+c-1 \;\Longrightarrow\; p(\tau)=q(\tau)\,\exp\!\big(w^\top\Phi(\tau)+c-1\big)$

The structure is already visible: the dynamics factor times the exponential of the reward. Only the constant $c$ remains.

Step 4 · Normalise — the partition function appears

Imposing $\sum_\tau p(\tau)=1$ gives $e^{c-1}=1/Z(w)$ with

$Z(w)\triangleq\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}}q(\tau)\,e^{w^\top\Phi(\tau)}$

The partition function is defined by normalisation, nothing more. But Z is a sum over the trajectory set — and that sum is the computational heart of the whole subject. We compute it in a few slides.
Act II · The result

The maximum-entropy trajectory model

$p_w(\tau) = \frac{1}{Z(w)}\,\underbrace{q(\tau)}_{\text{what nature does}}\;\underbrace{e^{w^\top\Phi(\tau)}}_{\text{what the agent wants}}$

Two trajectories with equal reward need not be equally likely: the one the environment resists is rarer.

Exponentially better trajectories are exponentially more likely, but never certain — precisely the graceful handling of a sub-optimal expert that max-margin lacked.

Contrast explicitly with max-margin: instead of "the expert is always best", we get "the expert is exponentially more likely to do better". That is Boltzmann rationality, and it is the behaviour model written down honestly.
Act II · The dual

The dual is maximum likelihood

Substituting $p_w$ back into the Lagrangian, the entropy term becomes $-w^\top\!\sum p\Phi+\log Z$, the normalisation term vanishes, and the feature term cancels the first piece, leaving

$g(w) \;\triangleq\; \log Z(w) - w^\top\mu_E$   (to be minimised)
Theorem

Minimising $g$ is exactly maximising the average log-likelihood of the demonstrations — up to a constant that does not involve $w$:

$\max_w\ \tfrac1m\sum_i\log p_w(\tau^{(i)}) = -\min_w g(w) + \underbrace{\tfrac1m\sum_i\log q(\tau^{(i)})}_{\text{constant, no }w}$

The dropped term is the log-likelihood of the observed transitions under the true dynamics — a constant of the optimisation, which is why it is usually invisible.

This is a pleasing and important identity: the maximum-entropy selection principle IS maximum likelihood on the exponential-family model. That is why MaxEnt inherits everything good about MLE: priors, regularisation, model comparison.
Act II · Why the dual is easy

Convexity, and the learning rule

Gradient of $\log Z$

$\nabla_w Z = \sum_\tau q(\tau)e^{w^\top\Phi}\Phi \;\Longrightarrow\; \nabla_w\log Z(w)=\mathbb{E}_{p_w}[\Phi(\tau)]$

Hessian is a covariance

$\nabla_w^2\log Z(w)=\mathrm{Cov}_{p_w}[\Phi(\tau)]\succeq0$, so $g$ is convex — strictly so precisely when the features are not confined to a proper affine subspace.

The learning rule — remember this

$-\nabla_w g(w) \;=\; \mu_E - \mathbb{E}_{p_w}[\Phi(\tau)]$

Gradient ascent closes the gap between what the expert did and what the current model predicts; at the optimum the two coincide.

The learning rule is the whole algorithm in one line. But notice the innocent term E_{p_w}[Phi] on the right: evaluating it requires Z(w), a sum over trajectories. The entire rest of the lecture exists because of that term.
Act II · A tension worth noticing

Unique estimator, unidentified estimand

Both are true

The reward parameter $w$ has a unique optimum (convexity, above).
The reward function is not identified (Act I: the cone, the shaping ambiguity).

Resolution: maximum entropy is a selection principle — it picks one point out of the equivalence class, and picks it uniquely.

Uniqueness of an estimator is not identifiability of an estimand.

This resolves a confusion that frustrates almost every beginner. Keep this slide — it is short but it does real work.
Act II · Coordinate 2, made concrete

Computing $Z$: enumeration is hopeless

The learning rule looks like a two-line algorithm. Evaluating $\mathbb{E}_{p_w}[\Phi]$ requires $Z(w)$, and $Z(w)$ is a sum over $\mathcal{T}$.

$|\mathcal{T}|$ grows like $|\mathcal{A}|^T$: a $5\times5$ gridworld, 4 actions, $T=20$ gives $4^{20}\approx10^{12}$ trajectories.

Finite — and still impossible.

Why bother with the discrete formulation at all?

The value is not that we enumerate; it is that $Z$ is thereby unambiguously defined, so we can look for structure. And the structure is that $q$ factorises.

This is the pivot from Coordinate 1 to Coordinate 2. Everything from here to Act IV is about that one expectation.
Act II · $q$ factorises · the whole trick

$Z$ by a backward recursion

Because $q(\tau)=d_0(s_0)\prod_tP(s_{t+1}\mid s_t,a_t)$ and $\Phi(\tau)=\sum_t\phi(s_t,a_t)$, the sum over trajectories telescopes into a recursion over states.

Backward recursion

Let $Z_T(s)\triangleq1$. For $t=T-1,\dots,0$:

$Z_t(s) = \sum_{a} e^{R_w(s,a)}\sum_{s'}P(s'\mid s,a)\,Z_{t+1}(s')$

Then $Z(w)=\sum_s d_0(s)\,Z_0(s)$.

In logs: the soft Bellman backup

With $V_t(s)=\log Z_t(s)$:

$V_t(s)=\log\sum_a\exp\Big(R_w(s,a)+\underbrace{\log\textstyle\sum_{s'}P(s'\mid s,a)e^{V_{t+1}(s')}}_{=:\,Q_t(s,a)}\Big)$

Cost: $O(T|\mathcal{S}|^2|\mathcal{A}|)$ instead of enumerating all $O(|\mathcal{A}|^{T})$ trajectories.

Emphasise the telescoping: splitting off the first action and successor gives the recursion; the base case is the empty suffix. Averaging over s_0 ~ d_0 gives Z. This IS a soft (entropy-regularised) Bellman backup — the same object that reappears as soft Q-learning and SAC.
Act II · Counting the uses of the model

Where does knowledge of $P$ enter? (1 of 2)

Look at the backward recursion. The kernel $P(s'\mid s,a)$ is consumed inside the inner sum, once per $(s,a)$ per timestep.

Remember this

This is the first of exactly two places where MaxEnt IRL needs a model. The second appears when we compute the expected features in the forward pass.

When Act III asks “what if $P$ is unknown?”, these two sums are the entire content of the question.

Plant this explicitly. The "two consumers of P" framing makes Exit C tractable instead of mysterious.

Act II · The third subtle point

The stochastic-dynamics trap

The example was deterministic — and that was not laziness. Look at the inner term of the recursion:

$\log\sum_{s'} P(s'\mid s,a)\,e^{V_{t+1}(s')}$

This is a soft maximum over successor states, not an expectation. By Jensen, $\log\mathbb{E}[e^X]\ge\mathbb{E}[X]$, so it systematically over-values actions with high variance in their outcomes.

Proposition · the model rewrites the dynamics

$p_w(s_{t+1}=s'\mid s,a) = \frac{P(s'\mid s,a)e^{V_{t+1}(s')}}{\sum_{s''}P(s''\mid s,a)e^{V_{t+1}(s'')}} \;\ne\; P(s'\mid s,a)$

The naive model is not a model of an agent choosing actions in the given MDP — it is a model of an agent who also tilts nature towards fortunate outcomes. It will explain lucky outcomes as intentional and infer rewards that reward luck.

In a deterministic MDP the tilt is vacuous — which is why the original presentation is correct and the problem is so easy to miss.

This is subtle point 3 of the four the handout insists on. The fix is on the next slide: optimise over POLICIES, so the true dynamics appears as an unchangeable factor.
Act II · The remedy

Maximum causal entropy

Count only the agent’s own choices as uncertainty; randomness supplied by nature is not credited to the agent:

$\mathcal{H}^{\mathrm{causal}}(\pi)=\mathbb{E}_{p_\pi}\Big[\textstyle\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}-\log\pi_t(a_t\mid s_t)\Big]$

Theorem: maximising this subject to $\mathbb{E}_{p_\pi}[\Phi]=\mu_E$ gives the soft-optimal policy for $R_w$, via

$Q_t(s,a)=R_w(s,a)+\sum_{s'}P(s'\mid s,a)V_{t+1}(s'),\quad V_t(s)=\log\sum_a e^{Q_t(s,a)},\quad \pi_t(a\mid s)=e^{Q_t(s,a)-V_t(s)}$

The dual stays convex and the gradient stays $\mu_E-\mathbb{E}_{p_\pi}[\Phi]$.

Ziebart's 2010 thesis is the correct treatment of stochastic dynamics. Attribution matters: the 2008 AAAI paper is right for the deterministic setting it considers.
Act II · The remedy, side by side

The one-symbol difference

Naive MaxEnt

$\log\sum_a\exp\Big(R + \log\textstyle\sum_{s'} P\,e^{V}\Big)$ — soft max over both actions and successor states

Maximum causal entropy

$\log\sum_a\exp\Big(R + \sum_{s'} P\,V\Big)$ — soft max over actions, plain expectation over successor states

They coincide exactly when $P$ is deterministic. Every modern method — soft $Q$-learning, SAC, GAIL’s entropy term, AIRL — uses the causal version.

When a paper says “MaxEnt IRL”, it almost always means the causal version.

Put the two formulas side by side and let the audience find the difference themselves: log-sum-exp over successors replaced by an expectation. One symbol, enormous consequences.
Act II · From a policy to expected features

The forward pass

The backward pass produced a policy. To get $\mathbb{E}_{p_\pi}[\Phi]$ we must know where that policy goes. Let $d_t(s)=\mathrm{Pr}_{p_\pi}(s_t=s)$:

$d_{t+1}(s') = \sum_{s}\sum_{a} d_t(s)\,\pi_t(a\mid s)\,P(s'\mid s,a)$
$\mathbb{E}_{p_\pi}[\Phi] = \sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\sum_{s,a} d_t(s)\,\pi_t(a\mid s)\,\phi(s,a)$
Where does $P$ enter? (2 of 2, and the last)

The backward pass computes what the agent would do; the forward pass computes where doing it takes them. Both need the kernel; neither needs anything else.

Two passes, two consumers of P. That is the complete inventory — which is exactly what makes Exit C a well-posed question.
Act II · Naming the weighting

The occupancy measure

$\rho_\pi(s,a) \triangleq (1-\gamma)\sum_{t=0}^\infty \gamma^t\,\mathrm{Pr}_\pi(s_t=s,a_t=a)$

a probability distribution over $\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}$. Then return is a linear functional of occupancy:

$J_R(\pi)=\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\sum_{s,a}\rho_\pi(s,a)R(s,a), \qquad \mu(\pi)=\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\sum_{s,a}\rho_\pi(s,a)\phi(s,a)$
Occupancy is not action frequency

$\rho_\pi$ is a distribution over pairs. “Brake” in front of a pedestrian and “brake” on an empty motorway are different events because their states differ.

Act IV is built entirely on the sentence “return is linear in $\rho$”.

The occupancy measure is the single most useful change of variable in imitation learning. It converts "match behaviour" into "match a distribution", which is a problem statistics knows how to attack.
Act II · Putting it together

The complete exact algorithm

Exact Maximum Causal Entropy IRL (finite $\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A}$, known $P$)
  1. $\mu_E \gets \frac1m\sum_i\Phi(\tau^{(i)})$;  initialise $w\gets0$.
  2. Repeat until $\|\mu_E-\hat\mu\|$ is small:
  3. a. $R_w(s,a)\gets w^\top\phi(s,a)$.
  4. b. Backward pass: $V_T\equiv0$; for $t=T-1,\dots,0$ compute $Q_t,V_t,\pi_t$.  [uses $P$]
  5. c. Forward pass: propagate $d_{t+1}$ from $d_0$.  [uses $P$]
  6. d. $\hat\mu\gets\sum_{t,s,a}d_t(s)\pi_t(a\mid s)\phi(s,a)$.
  7. e. $w\gets w+\eta(\mu_E-\hat\mu)$.
  8. Return $w$ and the soft-optimal policy $\pi$.

Per iteration: $O(T|\mathcal{S}|^2|\mathcal{A}|)$ — linear in the horizon, quadratic in the states. That quadratic is what will not survive contact with a real problem.

Convex objective, diminishing steps, global convergence. This is a complete, correct, globally convergent IRL method — and it is unusable on any problem anyone actually cares about, for three separate reasons. On to Act III.
Act II · Ledger

Exact MaxEnt / MaxCausalEnt IRL — the ledger

Assumed (Coordinate 1). The expert is Boltzmann-rational: exponentially prefers better trajectories rather than always choosing the best. Reward linear in fixed $\phi$. Among all models matching $\mu_E$, take the maximum-causal-entropy one.

Approximated (Coordinate 2). Nothing. $Z$ is exact via the backward pass; $\mathbb{E}[\Phi]$ is exact via the forward pass. Requires finite $\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A}$ and the kernel $P$ in closed form.

Delivers. A unique $w$ (convex problem), a stochastic policy that degrades gracefully with expert noise, and a likelihood — so the model can be compared, regularised and given priors, none of which max-margin allowed.

Breaks when. $|\mathcal{S}|$ is large or continuous; the horizon is infinite; or $P$ is unknown. These are the three exits of Act III.

This ledger is the reference point for the rest of the lecture. Each exit removes one assumption; each adversarial method repairs one of the resulting deficits.

Act III · Exit A · infinite horizon

Why trajectory entropy dies

The finite-horizon derivation does not survive $T\to\infty$ as written — and the reason is not the return, it is the entropy.

The divergence

$\mathcal{H}^{\mathrm{causal}}(\pi)=\mathbb{E}\big[\sum_{t<T}-\log\pi(a_t\mid s_t)\big]$ grows linearly in $T$ for any non-deterministic policy — so the objective diverges.

The repair is the same discounting we already apply to reward:

$\mathcal{H}_\gamma(\pi)=\mathbb{E}_\pi\Big[\textstyle\sum_{t=0}^\infty\gamma^t\big(-\log\pi(a_t\mid s_t)\big)\Big]$
$J_R^\beta(\pi) \;\triangleq\; J_R(\pi) + \beta\,\mathcal{H}_\gamma(\pi)$

The temperature $\beta>0$ is now explicit; Act II used $\beta=1$.

Note the parallel structure: we discount rewards because far-future rewards should matter less, and we discount entropy for exactly the same reason. The two discounts must agree, or the objective is not well-defined as T grows.
Act III · Exit A · the contraction survives

The soft Bellman operator

$(\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{soft}}Q)(s,a) = R(s,a) + \gamma\,\mathbb{E}_{s'\sim P(\cdot\mid s,a)}\Big[\beta\log\textstyle\sum_{a'}\exp\big(Q(s',a')/\beta\big)\Big]$

Lemma (log-sum-exp is a non-expansion). For $x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{A}|}$ and $\beta>0$:

$\Big|\beta\log\textstyle\sum_a e^{x_a/\beta} - \beta\log\sum_a e^{y_a/\beta}\Big| \;\le\; \|x-y\|_\infty$

Theorem. $\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{soft}}$ is a $\gamma$-contraction in $\|\cdot\|_\infty$: unique fixed point $Q_{\mathrm{soft}}$, geometric convergence, and the soft-optimal policy $\pi_{\mathrm{soft}}(a\mid s)\propto\exp(Q_{\mathrm{soft}}(s,a)/\beta)$ maximises $J_R^\beta$.

A finite backward sweep becomes a stationary fixed point. Nothing statistical changes.

The proof is on the next slide. The headline: the contraction survives softening, so infinite-horizon MaxEnt is as well-posed as the finite case — only the interpretation of log Z changes.
Act III · Proof, in three steps

log-sum-exp is a non-expansion

Step 1 · Bound coordinate by coordinate

Let $\delta=\|x-y\|_\infty$. Then $x_a\le y_a+\delta$ for every $a$.

Step 2 · Exponentiate and sum

$\sum_a e^{x_a/\beta} \;\le\; e^{\delta/\beta}\sum_a e^{y_a/\beta}$

Step 3 · Take $\beta\log$ and symmetrise

$\beta\log\sum_a e^{x_a/\beta} \le \beta\log\sum_a e^{y_a/\beta} + \delta$. Exchanging $x$ and $y$ gives the reverse inequality, so $|\cdot|\le\delta$.  ∎

This is the technical reason soft RL is safe: monotonicity of exp plus the infinity-norm bound. Contrast with the deadly triad: function approximation plus bootstrapping plus off-policy can destroy exactly this contraction.
Act III · Exit A · what $\beta$ means

The temperature interpolates

$\max_a Q(s,a) \;\le\; \beta\log\textstyle\sum_a e^{Q(s,a)/\beta} \;\le\; \max_a Q(s,a) + \beta\log|\mathcal{A}|$

The soft operator is within $\beta\log|\mathcal{A}|$ of $\mathcal{B}^\star$ everywhere.

$\beta$ is a Coordinate-1 modelling choice

As $\beta\to0$ we recover hard optimality — max-margin’s behaviour model.
As $\beta\to\infty$ the policy becomes uniform and the demonstrations are treated as uninformative.
$\beta$ says how rational you think the expert is.

Fitting $\beta$ from data is possible but entangled with the scale of $R$: $(R,\beta)$ and $(cR,c\beta)$ give the same policy — one more instance of the identifiability theme.

This is a good interactive moment: ask the audience what beta = 0 and beta = infinity mean before revealing. It frames temperature as an assumption, not a hyperparameter.
Act III · Exit A · large or continuous $\mathcal{S}$

Here the loss is real

Both passes are tabular sweeps costing $O(|\mathcal{S}|^2|\mathcal{A}|)$. Three things break at once:

1 · The tables do not fit

$V$, $\pi$ and $d_t$ are arrays indexed by $\mathcal{S}$; for continuous $\mathcal{S}$ they are functions and must be approximated — reintroducing every hazard of the deadly triad into the inner loop of IRL.

2 · Discretisation does not save you

An $\varepsilon$-net of $[0,1]^n$ has $\varepsilon^{-n}$ cells: a 10-dimensional state at resolution $0.1$ is $10^{10}$ cells, and the sweep is quadratic in that.

3 · The reference measure becomes load-bearing

$\sum_\tau q(\tau)(\cdot)$ becomes $\int(\cdot)\,dq(\tau)$. Here $-\mathrm{KL}(p\|q)$ stays well-defined and reparameterisation-invariant; $-\int p\log p$ is neither. Had we begun with differential entropy, the objective would change value under a smooth change of coordinates — an absurdity for a principle encoding ignorance.

Point 3 pays off the investment in the reference measure from Act II. This is why we insisted on q from the very first line even though it was a harmless constant in the discrete case.
Act III · Exit A · continuous actions

An intractable normaliser, solved variationally

The soft max over actions becomes an integral with no closed form:

$\beta\log\sum_a\exp(Q/\beta) \;\longrightarrow\; \beta\log\int_{\mathcal{A}}\exp\big(Q(s,a)/\beta\big)\,da$

The standard resolution is variational: represent the policy explicitly and fit it to the Boltzmann target:

$\pi \;\leftarrow\; \operatorname*{argmin}_{\pi'\in\Pi_\theta}\; \mathrm{KL}\Big(\pi'(\cdot\mid s)\,\Big\|\,\tfrac{1}{Z(s)}\exp\big(Q(s,\cdot)/\beta\big)\Big)$

— exactly the policy-improvement step of soft actor–critic.

Note the structure — we will meet it again one level up

An intractable normaliser over actions is replaced by a learned sampler. Next: an intractable normaliser over trajectories is replaced by a learned policy.

This is the conceptual seed of GAIL. Say it out loud: the trick that makes SAC work on continuous actions is the same trick that will make GAIL work on continuous trajectories.

Act III · Exit B · must features be hand-crafted?

What a fixed linear class buys

1 · A sufficient statistic

$\mu(\pi)\in\mathbb{R}^d$ summarises the policy for all reward-evaluation purposes. An infinite-dimensional object collapses to $d$ numbers.

2 · Convexity

$\log Z$ needs $R$ linear in the parameter. This is what makes MaxEnt IRL a well-behaved convex estimation problem.

3 · A class-wide guarantee

Feature matching certifies performance for every reward in the class at once — far stronger than certifying it for one estimate.

4 · Interpretability

“The expert weights collision risk 8 times lane deviation” is a sentence one can act on.

Short answer to Exit B: no, features need not be hand-crafted, and modern methods do not hand-craft them. But the useful question is what fixed phi was DOING for us.
Act III · Exit B · the cost

Misspecification is invisible from inside

The trap

Every guarantee above is quantified over $w\in\mathbb{R}^d$ with $\phi$ fixed. If the true reward is not in $\mathrm{span}\{\phi_1,\dots,\phi_d\}$, matching $\mu_E$ implies nothing about $J_{R^\star}$.

Worse, the fit will look fine: the residual lives in a direction the model cannot see.

Classical apprenticeship-learning results on driving worked because a human had already encoded the task into features such as “distance to nearest car” and “off-road indicator”. The domain knowledge was in $\phi$, not the algorithm.

This is the honest statement of what feature engineering bought, and why deep IRL was such a relief — and why it immediately reintroduced every problem convexity had solved.
Act III · Exit B · a new ambiguity

Learning $\phi$ adds an ambiguity

Suppose we learn $\phi_\eta$ and $w$ jointly. For any invertible $A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$:

$w^\top\phi(s,a) = (A^{-\top}w)^\top\big(A\phi(s,a)\big)$

so $(w,\phi)$ and $(A^{-\top}w,A\phi)$ are indistinguishable.

Only the scalar field $R$ and the span of the features are meaningful; individual coordinates of $w$ are not. “The model learned that feature 3 matters” is meaningless without a convention pinning down $A$.

A Coordinate-1 ambiguity added by learning features, on top of the reward ambiguity we already had.

This matters practically: any interpretability claim about learned feature weights must state how the basis is fixed. Interpretable AI claims in IRL often ignore this.
Act III · Exit B · the good news

The learning rule does not change

One might fear that abandoning linearity forces a new derivation. It does not.

Proposition · MaxEnt gradient for any reward class

Let $R_\theta$ be any differentiable reward parameterisation, $p_\theta(\tau)\propto q(\tau)e^{R_\theta(\tau)}$. Then

$\nabla_\theta L(\theta) = \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\mathcal{D}_E}\big[\nabla_\theta R_\theta(\tau)\big]}_{\text{expert}} - \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim p_\theta}\big[\nabla_\theta R_\theta(\tau)\big]}_{\text{current model}}$

Proof

$\nabla_\theta\log Z(\theta) = \frac{1}{Z}\sum_\tau q(\tau)e^{R_\theta(\tau)}\nabla_\theta R_\theta(\tau) = \mathbb{E}_{p_\theta}\big[\nabla_\theta R_\theta(\tau)\big]$  ∎

This is the old rule with $\phi$ replaced by $\nabla_\theta R_\theta$; in the linear case $\nabla_wR_w(\tau)=\Phi(\tau)$ and they coincide exactly.

Feature matching was never the principle — matching the sufficient statistics of the reward class was.

This single observation makes deep MaxEnt IRL (Wulfmeier et al. 2015) and guided cost learning (Finn et al. 2016) possible with no new theory. It is the most re-assuring slide of Exit B.
Act III · Exit B · what is lost

Convexity, and the need for inductive bias

With $R_\theta$ nonlinear, $L$ is no longer concave: local optima, sensitivity to initialisation, and — most importantly — capacity to overfit.

The overfitting failure mode

A sufficiently expressive $R_\theta$ can memorise the demonstrations: huge reward exactly on the observed pairs, zero elsewhere. That reward perfectly explains the data and is useless.

Useful counter-pressures — all Coordinate-1 commitments:

  • Architectural restriction — most powerfully, $R_\theta(s)$ depending on state only. (Not a convenience: a necessary condition for transfer in Act IV.)
  • Smoothness and norm penalties; gradient penalties on a discriminator.
  • Bottlenecks on the reward representation.
  • Multi-task structure, restoring identifiability of the shared part.
  • Priors — Bayesian IRL, making the selection principle explicit.
The reward-class choice never disappears when features are learned; it becomes the choice of ARCHITECTURE. And in Act IV it becomes the choice of DISCRIMINATOR — which is why we insist a discriminator architecture is a reward class in disguise.
Act III · Exit C · what if we do not know $P$?

First: separate the two jobs

Job 1 · estimate the expert’s statistics $\rho_E$

Data: the demonstrations. Needs no model and no interaction — an average over $\mathcal{D}_E$. Its weakness is support: it says nothing outside the states the expert visited.

Job 2 · estimate the current candidate policy’s statistics $\rho_{\pi_w}$

Not in the data at all — $\pi_w$ changes every gradient step. Requires either a model of $P$ (to compute) or interaction (to sample). This is the only job that needs $P$.

Recall: $P$ is consumed in exactly two places, both belonging to Job 2 — the successor expectation in the backward pass, and the visitation propagation in the forward pass. Everything below is about replacing those two sums.

This separation dissolves most beginner confusion about model-free IRL. Most people conflate "we don't know P" with "we can't compute anything". Job 1 is trivial; Job 2 is the whole problem.

Act III · Exit C · the Monte Carlo replacement

Sampling the gradient

Draw $n$ rollouts $\tau_1,\dots,\tau_n$ from the current soft policy:

$\widehat{\mathbb{E}_{p_w}[\Phi]} = \frac1n\sum_j\Phi(\tau_j), \qquad \widehat{\nabla_w L} = \mu_E - \frac1n\sum_j\Phi(\tau_j)$

Unbiased and consistent. The catch: the current soft policy must itself be obtained by an inner forward-RL solve at every outer step — extremely wasteful, since $w$ changes only slightly.

Guided cost learning (Finn et al. 2016) removes the waste

Importance sampling: maintain a sampler $\pi_{\mathrm{samp}}$, reuse its rollouts, correct with weights

$\omega_j \propto \frac{e^{R_\theta(\tau_j)}}{\pi_{\mathrm{samp}}(\tau_j)}, \qquad \widehat{\nabla_\theta L} = \mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_E}[\nabla_\theta R_\theta] - \sum_j\frac{\omega_j}{\sum_{j'}\omega_{j'}}\nabla_\theta R_\theta(\tau_j)$

The dynamics terms cancel in the ratio — one never needs $P$ — but the weights degenerate when $\pi_{\mathrm{samp}}$ drifts from $p_\theta$.

Guided cost learning is the bridge from exact MaxEnt to the adversarial methods: it keeps the MaxEnt objective but replaces the partition function with importance-weighted samples.
Act III · Exit C · the three prices

Support, exploration, and model error

Support

A transition never attempted cannot be estimated. If $\rho_E$ has support the learner never visits (or vice versa), the comparison driving the gradient is being made on incomparable sets — the deep reason imitation degrades off the expert’s distribution, and the origin of compounding error in behavioural cloning.

Model error compounds

Fit $\widehat{P}$ and plan in it, and errors accumulate over the effective horizon: per-step total-variation error $\varepsilon$ gives occupancy error $\varepsilon/(1-\gamma)$ and hence a biased reward gradient. Bias does not average away over gradient steps the way variance does.

Interaction may be unacceptable

Exploration in surgery, aviation or finance has costs that no sample-complexity bound captures.

These are the practical caveats to the "just sample it" answer. The decision rule: demand exact transition probabilities only when the algorithm asks for exact dynamic-programming expectations.
Act III · Exit C · the idea that generates everything after

The pivot: from an expectation to a ratio

The gradient asks us to compare two expectations of a known function $\phi$ under two unknown distributions, $\rho_E$ and $\rho_\pi$.

We have samples from both: the demonstrations give samples of $\rho_E$; rollouts give samples of $\rho_\pi$. So the real task is:

Given samples from two distributions, quantify and reduce their discrepancy.

Machine learning has an extremely well-developed tool for exactly that task — and it is not integration. It is binary classification.

A classifier trained to distinguish samples of $\rho_E$ from samples of $\rho_\pi$ recovers, at its optimum, the density ratio between them — and the density ratio is precisely the discrepancy signal we wanted.

No partition function. No transition kernel. No enumeration.

Slow down here. This is the single most important conceptual pivot in the lecture: everything in Act IV follows from replacing integration by classification. Say it, pause, then advance.
ACT IV · ADVERSARIAL METHODS

GAIL and AIRL

Not an unrelated import from generative modelling — the answers the three exits force on us.

Granular, one-idea-per-slide pacing continues through Act IV. Each idea gets its own slide and the derivations are revealed step by step, because this is where the correctness details actually bite.
Act IV · The right change of variables

Optimise over occupancies, not policies

Both Act-II objectives depended on the policy only through averages $\sum_{s,a}\rho_\pi(s,a)(\cdot)$. That is a strong hint that $\rho$, not $\pi$, is the natural variable.

Definition · the occupancy polytope

$\mathcal{D} \triangleq \Big\{\rho\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}}_{\ge0}:\ \sum_a\rho(s,a) = (1-\gamma)d_0(s) + \gamma\sum_{s',a'}P(s\mid s',a')\rho(s',a')\ \ \forall s\Big\}$

Non-negative measures satisfying the Bellman flow constraints.

Theorem · policies and occupancies are the same thing

$\pi\mapsto\rho_\pi$ is a bijection from stationary policies onto $\mathcal{D}$, with inverse $\pi_\rho(a\mid s)=\dfrac{\rho(s,a)}{\sum_{a'}\rho(s,a')}$. $\mathcal{D}$ is a compact convex polytope.

Classical: Puterman 1994, and Syed et al. 2008 for the LP formulation. Changing variables converts IRL into a convex-duality problem and makes GAIL a theorem rather than an analogy.
Act IV · Why the change of variables pays

Three consequences do all the work

1 · Return is linear in $\rho$

$J_R(\pi)=\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\langle R,\rho_\pi\rangle$. Forward RL is therefore a linear program over $\mathcal{D}$.

2 · Matching occupancy IS matching behaviour

$\rho_\pi=\rho_E$ if and only if $\pi=\pi_E$. Imitation is exactly a distribution-matching problem, with no reward mentioned.

3 · Mixtures are legal

$\mathcal{D}$ is convex, so $\rho_{\tilde\pi}=\sum_i\lambda_i\rho_{\pi_i}$ is realisable — which is what justified the mixed policy returned by max-margin.

Finally, discounted causal entropy is concave as a function of $\rho$: $\bar{\mathcal{H}}(\rho)=-\sum_{s,a}\rho(s,a)\log\frac{\rho(s,a)}{\sum_{a'}\rho(s,a')}$.

Consequence 2 is the philosophical heart of Act IV: imitation need not invoke reward at all. Consequence 3 retroactively explains the mixture output of apprenticeship learning.
Act IV · Ho & Ermon’s variational treatment

IRL with an explicit reward regulariser

A reward is good if the expert scores well under it and the best achievable policy does not score much better — i.e. if the reward explains the gap:

$\mathrm{IRL}_\psi(\pi_E) = \operatorname*{argmax}_R\ -\psi(R) + \underbrace{\Big(\min_\pi -\beta\bar{\mathcal{H}}(\rho_\pi) - \langle R,\rho_\pi\rangle\Big)}_{\text{best response to }R} + \langle R,\rho_E\rangle$

Without $\psi$ this is exactly the ill-posed problem of Act I. $\psi$ IS the selection principle — written down explicitly for once.

The inner min is "run RL on R"; the outer max is "pick R that makes the expert look good relative to the best response". The regulariser psi keeps the problem bounded, exactly as a norm constraint did for max-margin.
Act IV · Read this theorem slowly

RL ˆ IRL$_\psi$ = occupancy matching

Theorem (Ho & Ermon 2016)

Running RL on the reward returned by $\mathrm{IRL}_\psi$ yields

$\mathrm{RL}\circ\mathrm{IRL}_\psi(\pi_E) = \operatorname*{argmin}_\pi\ -\beta\bar{\mathcal{H}}(\rho_\pi) + \psi^*(\rho_\pi - \rho_E)$

where $\psi^*$ is the convex conjugate of $\psi$.

The whole IRL-then-RL pipeline is equivalent to a single occupancy-matching problem, in which the choice of reward regulariser $\psi$ becomes the choice of divergence $\psi^*$ used to compare $\rho_\pi$ with $\rho_E$.

The intermediate reward is a computational device that can be eliminated. Everything below is a choice of $\psi$.

Pause on this. It reframes IRL: reward is an auxiliary variable, and the real object is a divergence between occupancy measures. GAIL and apprenticeship learning are then just two different psi.
Act IV · The choice of regulariser

Two choices of $\psi$

(i) A constant on a linear class → apprenticeship learning

Let $\psi$ be $0$ if $R=w^\top\phi$ with $\|w\|_2\le1$ and $+\infty$ otherwise. Then $\psi^*$ is the support function of that set:

$\min_\pi\max_{\|w\|_2\le1} w^\top(\mu(\pi)-\mu_E) = \min_\pi\|\mu(\pi)-\mu_E\|_2$

Its weakness is now visible as a property of $\psi^*$: a hard constraint, exact feature matching within a small class, and every out-of-class $\rho_\pi$ treated as equally bad.

(ii) A smooth penalty on expert reward values → GAIL

$\psi_{\mathrm{GA}}(R)=\mathbb{E}_{\rho_E}[g(R(s,a))]$ for a particular convex $g$, whose conjugate is a Jensen–Shannon divergence:

$\psi_{\mathrm{GA}}^*(\rho_\pi-\rho_E) = \max_{D} \mathbb{E}_{\rho_E}[\log D] + \mathbb{E}_{\rho_\pi}[\log(1-D)]$

Penalises rewards that are large on expert pairs; is defined for all rewards; and crucially, its conjugate is computable from samples alone.

This slide retroactively explains why apprenticeship learning is brittle: its divergence is a hard indicator on a finite-dimensional class. GAIL's divergence is smooth, defined everywhere, and estimable from samples.
Act IV · GAIL (Ho & Ermon, 2016)

The GAIL objective

$\min_\pi\max_D\ \ \mathbb{E}_{(s,a)\sim\rho_E}\big[\log D(s,a)\big] + \mathbb{E}_{(s,a)\sim\rho_\pi}\big[\log\big(1-D(s,a)\big)\big] \;-\; \beta\,\bar{\mathcal{H}}(\rho_\pi)$

Convention — fixed for the rest of these slides

$D(s,a)$ is the estimated probability that the pair came from the expert.

Ho & Ermon’s paper uses the opposite labelling; the objectives are related by $D\mapsto1-D$. Mixing the two conventions is the single most common source of sign confusion in this literature.

State the convention clearly and never deviate. In practice: discriminator step ascends the log-likelihood (expert labelled 1, rollouts labelled 0); policy step runs TRPO/PPO on the surrogate reward.
Act IV · What the discriminator actually computes

The discriminator estimates a density ratio

Step 1 · For fixed $\pi$, the inner max is pointwise

For each $(s,a)$, maximise over $D\in(0,1)$:

$\rho_E\log D + \rho_\pi\log(1-D)$

A concave function of $D$ — two logs with positive weights.

Step 2 · Stationarity

$\dfrac{\rho_E}{D} - \dfrac{\rho_\pi}{1-D} = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; \rho_E(1-D)=\rho_\pi D$

Step 3 · The optimal discriminator

$D^\star(s,a) = \dfrac{\rho_E(s,a)}{\rho_E(s,a)+\rho_\pi(s,a)}$

The posterior probability of “expert”. Its log-odds recover the density ratio: $\log\frac{D^\star}{1-D^\star}=\log\frac{\rho_E}{\rho_\pi}$.

This is the key computation of Act IV. A classifier at its optimum is a density-ratio estimator — that is precisely the quantity the MaxEnt gradient needed, obtained without any partition function.
Act IV · Substituting back

GAIL minimises Jensen–Shannon divergence

Step 4 · The value at $D^\star$

With $m=\tfrac12(\rho_E+\rho_\pi)$:

$\rho_E\log\frac{\rho_E}{\rho_E+\rho_\pi}+\rho_\pi\log\frac{\rho_\pi}{\rho_E+\rho_\pi} = \mathrm{KL}(\rho_E\|m)+\mathrm{KL}(\rho_\pi\|m)-\log 4 = 2\,\mathrm{JS}(\rho_E\|\rho_\pi)-\log4$

So GAIL is exactly:

$\min_\pi\; 2\,\mathrm{JS}(\rho_E\|\rho_\pi) - \beta\,\bar{\mathcal{H}}(\rho_\pi)$

The intractable normaliser over trajectories has been replaced by a classification problem on samples. That substitution is what makes GAIL scale.

Verified numerically: the pointwise value at D* equals 2*JS - log 4 exactly. This is the theorem that justifies calling GAIL "imitation as density-ratio estimation".
Act IV · A practical warning about signs

Surrogate rewards are not interchangeable

The policy player minimises $\mathbb{E}_{\rho_\pi}[\log(1-D)]$, i.e. maximises the return of $\hat r(s,a)=-\log(1-D(s,a))$. Three surrogates appear in practice:

surrogaterangeimplicit bias
$-\log(1-D)$$(0,+\infty)$always positive: encourages surviving longer
$\log D$$(-\infty,0)$always negative: encourages terminating sooner
$\log D-\log(1-D)$$(-\infty,\infty)$the true log-odds; unbiased in sign

On tasks with variable episode length these change the learned behaviour markedly, independently of anything about imitation. A text that says “use the log-odds” and an equation that uses $-\log(1-D)$ are not describing the same algorithm.

AIRL uses the log-odds form — for structural reasons, not cosmetic ones.

This is common-error 6 on our list. If you implement GAIL and the agent behaves oddly on variable-length episodes, check the surrogate reward first.
Act IV · The fourth subtle point

GAIL delivers no reward

At convergence the discriminator contains NO reward information

If imitation succeeds, $\rho_\pi=\rho_E$, so $D^\star\equiv\tfrac12$ and the surrogate reward $-\log(1-D^\star)$ is the constant $\log2$.

A constant reward makes every policy optimal (Act I). There is therefore literally nothing to extract at the end of a successful GAIL run.

Any reward-like signal GAIL exhibits exists only during training, as a by-product of the current mismatch between learner and expert. It is a function of the optimisation trajectory, not of the task.

This — not a vague appeal to “shaping terms” — is the precise reason GAIL does not transfer.

This is one of the subtle points the handout insists on, and it answers the question everyone asks: "but GAIL has a discriminator, isn't that a reward?" No — not at the only moment it would be useful.
Act IV · Ledger

GAIL — the ledger

Assumed (Coordinate 1). Behaviour model: entropy-regularised optimality. Selection principle: the $\psi_{\mathrm{GA}}$ regulariser, equivalently “match occupancy in Jensen–Shannon divergence”. Reward class: whatever the discriminator architecture can express.

Approximated (Coordinate 2). Everything, and cheaply. $Z$ is never formed; $P$ is never written down; both occupancy measures are represented only by samples. Requires online interaction and inherits the instability of minimax optimisation.

Delivers. An imitating policy — often excellent, in continuous control from raw observations. Not a reward.

Therefore. Use GAIL when you want behaviour in the same MDP. If you want an objective you can re-optimise elsewhere, read on.

GAIL is computationally excellent and statistically silent about reward. That asymmetry is exactly what AIRL sets out to repair — with one more assumption.
Act IV · AIRL (Fu, Luo & Levine, 2018)

Where the discriminator’s form comes from

It is not an arbitrary architecture. Suppose the “expert” distribution is the maximum-entropy model $p_\theta(\tau)\propto q(\tau)e^{\sum_tf_\theta(s_t,a_t)}$, and the “fakes” come from the current policy $\pi$. The Bayes-optimal discriminator between them is

$D_\theta(s,a) = \frac{\exp f_\theta(s,a)}{\exp f_\theta(s,a) + \pi(a\mid s)}$

because the dynamics factors $q$ are common to both hypotheses and cancel.

Proposition · the AIRL reward is the logit minus a log-policy

$\hat r(s,a) = \log D_\theta - \log(1-D_\theta) = f_\theta(s,a) - \log\pi(a\mid s)$

Contrast with GAIL, where the discriminator is an unconstrained function. AIRL constrains it to the form implied by the MaxEnt model — that constraint is what forces a reward to appear.
Act IV · The $-\log\pi$ term is not noise

The entropy bonus appears automatically

Maximising $\mathbb{E}_\pi\big[\sum_t\gamma^t\big(f-\log\pi\big)\big]$ is exactly maximising $J_f(\pi)+\mathcal{H}_\gamma(\pi)$.

So the adversarial game performs entropy-regularised RL on $f$

— whether or not anyone adds an entropy term by hand.

At the optimum, $f_\theta$ recovers the advantage of the expert’s soft policy:

$A_{\mathrm{soft}}(s,a) = Q_{\mathrm{soft}}(s,a) - V_{\mathrm{soft}}(s)$

And this is the crux of what follows: an advantage is a reward plus shaping, not a reward.

This observation is why AIRL needs the shaped form on the next slide. If the discriminator recovered the reward directly, no decomposition would be needed. It recovers an ADVANTAGE — which is shaped by construction.
Act IV · The structural constraint

$f$ is restricted to a shaped form

$f_{\theta,\omega}(s,a,s') = \underbrace{g_\theta(s)}_{\text{task reward}} \;+\; \underbrace{\gamma h_\omega(s') - h_\omega(s)}_{\text{potential-based shaping}}$

$g_\theta$ must depend on the state ONLY — this is the load-bearing assumption

It is very often mis-stated as $g_\theta(s,a)$. For fixed dynamics, the rewards inducing the same soft-optimal policy are exactly

$r'(s,a) = r(s,a) + \gamma\,\mathbb{E}_{s'\sim P(\cdot\mid s,a)}[\Psi(s')] - \Psi(s)$

for some potential $\Psi$. Note the shaping term depends on $a$ and depends on $P$.

This is one of the subtle points the handout insists on (error 8 on the common-errors list). Write the shaping term and point at its a-dependence and its P-dependence: those are exactly the two things that block transfer.
Act IV · Why state-only is required

A state–action reward cannot transfer

The shaping term $\gamma\mathbb{E}_{s'\mid s,a}[\Psi(s')]-\Psi(s)$ is a nuisance that (i) cannot be distinguished from the reward by any amount of behavioural data, and (ii) changes when the dynamics change.

(ii) is precisely a failure of transfer.

If instead $g$ is a function of $s$ alone, then the only member of the family that is state-only is $\Psi\equiv\mathrm{const}$ (under the decomposability condition). The action-dependent freedom is squeezed entirely into $h_\omega$, and $g_\theta$ is pinned down up to an additive constant.

Definition · decomposability

Two states are one-step linked if some state has positive-probability transitions to both. The dynamics are decomposable if the transitive closure of this relation connects all of $\mathcal{S}$.

The decomposability condition rules out disconnected components of the state space, where a state-only function and a shaping function could trade off undetectably.
Act IV · The payoff

The disentanglement theorem

Theorem (Fu et al. 2018)

Suppose the dynamics are decomposable, the discriminator is trained to optimality with $f$ constrained as above, and the true expert is soft-optimal for a state-only reward $r^\star(s)$. Then at the global optimum there is a constant $c$ with

$g_\theta(s) = r^\star(s) + c, \qquad h_\omega(s) = V^\star_{\mathrm{soft}}(s) + c$

In particular $g_\theta$ is invariant to changes in the transition dynamics — so re-optimising $g_\theta$ in a different MDP with the same reward structure recovers appropriate behaviour there.

Two bonuses: $g_\theta$ is the transferable reward, and $h_\omega$ is a soft value-function estimate — useful in its own right.

This is the second time in the lecture we have bought something with an assumption: max-margin bought a guarantee with "expert is optimal"; AIRL buys transfer with "the reward is state-only and the dynamics are decomposable".
Act IV · What the theorem does and does not say

Read the assumptions as the price

It says: under a structural condition on the dynamics, a state-only reward assumption, and exact optimisation, the recovered $g$ equals the ground-truth reward up to a constant.

It does NOT say the reward is uniquely recoverable in general.

The state-only restriction is doing the identification

It is an assumption about the world, not a fact derived from data. If the true reward genuinely depends on actions (an energy cost, say), AIRL will misattribute that dependence into $h_\omega$ and the recovered $g$ will be wrong.

Act I has not been repealed; it has been paid off with an extra assumption — and the whole point of this lecture is that this is always the trade.

Say this firmly. It guards against the most common overclaim about AIRL. Also note the optimisation caveat: the theorem is about the global optimum of a minimax problem, which no optimiser reliably finds.
Act IV · Ledger

AIRL — the ledger

Assumed (Coordinate 1). Everything GAIL assumes, plus: the true reward is state-only, and the dynamics are decomposable. In exchange, the shaping ambiguity — the one ambiguity that blocks transfer — is quotiented out by construction.

Approximated (Coordinate 2). As GAIL: samples throughout, no $Z$, no $P$. Additionally requires evaluating $\pi(a\mid s)$ explicitly inside $D$, so the policy must have a tractable density.

Delivers. $g_\theta\approx r^\star+c$, re-optimisable under new dynamics; $h_\omega\approx V^\star_{\mathrm{soft}}$ as a useful by-product.

Fails when. The reward really depends on actions; the dynamics are not decomposable; or the discriminator is not trained near optimality.

Compare side by side with the GAIL ledger two slides back: identical Coordinate-2 column, one extra line in Coordinate-1, and "delivers a reward” instead of "delivers only a policy". That one line costs one assumption.

Act V · The whole history in one paragraph

The one-paragraph version

Max-margin fixed the ambiguity geometrically and paid with a brittle behaviour model.

Maximum entropy replaced the geometry with a likelihood, which handled noise and gave a convex problem, and paid with an intractable partition function.

Occupancy measures revealed that both were solving a distribution-matching problem in disguise.

GAIL then estimated that distributional discrepancy by classification instead of integration, which made it scale — at the cost of throwing the reward away entirely.

AIRL put a reward back in by constraining the classifier so a state-only component and a shaping component are forced apart, buying transfer with one more assumption.

At no point did anyone recover a true reward from data alone, and at no point did anyone claim to.

If the audience remembers one slide from the whole lecture, this is the one to remember.
Act V · The three beginner questions

Q1 · “What happens in the real, infinite case?”

The statistics are unchanged; only the computation changes.

  • Infinite horizon: discounted causal entropy + a soft Bellman fixed point. Still a contraction — nothing is lost.
  • Large or continuous $\mathcal{S}$: tabular passes are destroyed; function approximation and sampling take over.
  • Continuous $\mathcal{A}$: the soft max over actions becomes an intractable integral, handled variationally.
  • On a continuum, the objective is well-posed only as a relative entropy $-\mathrm{KL}(p\|q)$ — which is why $q$ was there from line one.

We learn IRL discretely first not because discrete problems matter, but because there every quantity is definable — so you can see precisely which ones later become approximations.

This is the answer to the most common question in the Q&A. The framing "statistics unchanged, computation changes" usually satisfies it completely.
Act V · The three beginner questions

Q2 · “Do the features have to be hand-designed?”

No. The learning rule $\mathbb{E}_{\text{expert}}[\nabla_\theta R_\theta]-\mathbb{E}_{\text{model}}[\nabla_\theta R_\theta]$ holds for any differentiable reward class; $\mu_E-\mathbb{E}[\Phi]$ is the special case $R_\theta=w^\top\phi$.

What fixed linear $\phi$ bought: a finite sufficient statistic, a convex dual, a class-wide guarantee, interpretability. What it cost: silent misspecification.

Learning $\phi$ trades convexity for expressiveness and adds the $(w,\phi)\sim(A^{-\top}w,A\phi)$ ambiguity.

Most importantly

The choice never disappears. In GAIL and AIRL, the discriminator architecture IS the reward class — performed less visibly.

Whenever a deep imitation learning paper claims interpretability, ask which architecture choice fixed the basis A.
Act V · The three beginner questions

Q3 · “What if we don’t know $P$?”

First separate the two jobs. Estimating the expert’s statistics needs no model at all. Estimating the current policy’s statistics is the only place $P$ was ever used — the successor expectation in the soft backward pass, and the visitation propagation in the forward pass.

Both are expectations, so both can be replaced by sampling whenever you can draw transitions — even if you can never write $P$ down.

You pay in variance, sample complexity, coverage, and possibly real-world exploration risk. You do not pay any change to the IRL objective itself.

Pushed one step further, the observation that we only ever needed to compare two sampled distributions replaces integration by classification — and that step is GAIL.

This question is where the "two consumers of P" inventory from Act II pays off. The answer is completely determined by that inventory.
Act V · For any IRL paper you read

A checklist

  1. What is the behaviour model? Exact optimality, Boltzmann rationality at which temperature, or something else?
  2. What is the reward class — including when it is hidden in an architecture?
  3. What is the selection principle, and is it stated or smuggled?
  4. Which quantity plays the role of $\mathbb{E}_{\text{model}}[\cdot]$, and how is it obtained — exact DP, rollouts, importance weights, or a discriminator?
  5. Does the method need $P$, a simulator, online interaction, or only offline data?
  6. What exactly is claimed to be recovered — a policy, a reward equivalence class, or a specific reward — and under what conditions?
  7. If transfer is claimed, which ambiguity was removed to make it possible, and by what assumption?
Tell the audience: if they remember nothing else, remember to fill in this ledger for any IRL paper they read. That is the transferable skill this lecture offers.
Act V · Mistakes that recur in secondary expositions

Common errors, collected

  1. Deriving $p(\tau)\propto e^{w^\top\Phi}$ and later asserting $p(\tau)\propto q(\tau)e^{w^\top\Phi}$. Different programs.
  2. Writing the program as $-\int p\log p$ on a continuous trajectory space.
  3. Using the naive soft backward pass under stochastic dynamics (risk-seeking; rewrites $P$).
  4. Attributing potential-based shaping to Ng & Russell (2000) — it is Ng, Harada & Russell (1999).
  5. Returning the last policy from Abbeel & Ng’s algorithm — the guarantee is for a mixture.
  6. Mixing GAIL discriminator conventions; treating $-\log(1-D)$, $\log D$ and the log-odds as interchangeable.
  7. Claiming GAIL learns a reward. At convergence $D\equiv\frac12$.
  8. Writing AIRL’s task term as $g_\theta(s,a)$ — the theorem requires state-only.
  9. Confusing uniqueness of $V^\star$ with identifiability of $R$; or uniqueness of the estimator $w$ with identifiability of the estimand.
Each of these is discussed in the handout with a reference. Several appeared in earlier drafts of these very notes — they are easy to make.
Act V · The takeaway

Every capability is purchased with an assumption.

IRL is easy to state and impossible to solve as stated. Read the field through its two failures and the history becomes a single argument — and the assumptions are always visible, if you look for them in the right two places.
